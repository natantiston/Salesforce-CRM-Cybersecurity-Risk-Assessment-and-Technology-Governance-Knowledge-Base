## 01.02 - Salesforce Platform Architecture and Multi-Tenant Infrastructure

## Part 1 - Foundations, Concepts and Technology Architecture

### 1. Introduction to Salesforce Platform Architecture

Salesforce is a cloud-based, multi-tenant application platform designed to support enterprise applications, customer relationship management, business automation, data management and digital services.

Understanding its architecture is essential for cybersecurity professionals because security responsibilities are distributed across multiple layers, including Salesforce-managed infrastructure, platform services, customer configurations, custom applications and external integrations.

Unlike a traditional on-premises application, where an organization may directly manage the application servers, operating systems, databases and network infrastructure, Salesforce operates the underlying platform as a managed cloud service.

Customers interact with the platform through supported interfaces, configuration capabilities, development tools and APIs. They remain responsible for securing their users, data access, configurations, custom applications and connected systems.

The Salesforce architecture should therefore be understood through five principal dimensions:

1. **Infrastructure architecture:** The underlying computing, storage, networking and data centre capabilities supporting Salesforce services.
2. **Platform architecture:** The shared application platform, runtime services, metadata framework and database capabilities.
3. **Application architecture:** Salesforce products, standard functionality, custom objects, business logic and user interfaces.
4. **Security architecture:** Identity, authentication, authorization, data protection, monitoring and platform security controls.
5. **Integration architecture:** APIs, middleware, connected applications and enterprise data exchange.

These dimensions collectively determine how Salesforce applications operate and how organizations should protect them.

#### 1.1.1 Architecture and Cybersecurity Relevance

A cybersecurity assessment must understand where a component operates, who manages it, what information it processes and which security controls apply.

For example:

- Salesforce manages the underlying infrastructure supporting its services.
- The customer organization configures users, permissions and application access.
- Developers are responsible for the security of custom application code.
- Integration teams protect API connections and connected systems.
- Security operations teams monitor available security events and investigate suspicious activities.
- Business and data owners define access requirements and acceptable risk.

A failure to understand these responsibilities can create security gaps.

An organization might assume that Salesforce automatically enforces every customer-specific access restriction, while the actual configuration may permit broader access than intended.

Similarly, an organization may assume that because Salesforce manages its infrastructure, the customer has no responsibility for application security, data governance or integration protection.

Both assumptions are incorrect.

### 2. Multi-Tenant Cloud Architecture

Multi-tenancy is a fundamental architectural characteristic of Salesforce.

A multi-tenant architecture allows multiple customers to use a shared cloud platform while maintaining logical separation of their respective environments, configurations and data.

Instead of operating a completely independent physical application infrastructure for every customer, the service provider operates shared infrastructure and platform services that support multiple customer organizations.

Each customer operates within its own Salesforce organization, commonly referred to as an **org**.

The org provides a logical environment for the customer's applications, users, data, configuration and business processes.

#### 2.1.1 Single-Tenant vs Multi-Tenant Architecture

**Single-Tenant Architecture**

In a traditional single-tenant deployment, a customer may have a dedicated application environment and supporting infrastructure.

Depending on the deployment model, the customer or service provider may manage separate application instances, databases and infrastructure resources.

Characteristics include:

- Dedicated application environment.
- Greater infrastructure isolation at the instance level.
- Potentially separate database deployment.
- Customer-specific infrastructure configuration.
- Higher infrastructure management requirements in some deployment models.

**Multi-Tenant Architecture**

In a multi-tenant deployment, multiple customers share underlying platform resources while their application environments and data remain logically separated.

Characteristics include:

- Shared underlying infrastructure.
- Shared platform services.
- Separate customer organizations.
- Logical separation of customer data.
- Standardized platform operations.
- Centralized service maintenance and upgrades.

| Architectural Dimension | Single-Tenant Model | Salesforce Multi-Tenant Model |
|---|---|---|
| Infrastructure | Dedicated environment may be used | Shared underlying infrastructure |
| Application Platform | Separate application instance may be deployed | Shared platform services |
| Customer Environment | Dedicated application environment | Separate Salesforce org |
| Data Separation | Often based on separate infrastructure or databases | Logical separation within the platform architecture |
| Platform Maintenance | Managed separately for each deployment | Managed centrally by Salesforce |
| Customization | Depends on deployment design | Supported through configuration, metadata and development capabilities |
| Infrastructure Responsibility | Depends on hosting and operating model | Salesforce manages the underlying service infrastructure |
| Customer Security Responsibility | Depends on service arrangement | Customer manages its users, data access, configurations and custom applications |

#### 2.1.2 Logical Separation of Customer Organizations

Each Salesforce customer operates within a logically distinct org.

An org contains customer-specific elements such as:

- User accounts.
- Business data.
- Standard and custom objects.
- Application configuration.
- Profiles and permission assignments.
- Sharing settings.
- Automation.
- Custom metadata.
- Custom application components.
- Integration configurations.

The org provides the logical boundary within which the customer's Salesforce environment is administered.

However, logical separation must not be confused with a dedicated physical infrastructure boundary.

The underlying platform remains shared.

Security therefore depends on Salesforce's platform isolation mechanisms and the correct implementation of customer-side access controls.

#### 2.1.3 Multi-Tenancy and Resource Sharing

The Salesforce platform uses shared resources to support multiple customer organizations.

These resources may include:

- Computing infrastructure.
- Application runtime services.
- Database infrastructure.
- Storage services.
- Platform management services.
- Network infrastructure.
- Operational services.

Resource sharing allows the provider to operate the service at enterprise scale.

However, shared infrastructure creates architectural requirements concerning:

- Tenant isolation.
- Data separation.
- Resource allocation.
- Performance management.
- Platform availability.
- Secure execution of customer operations.
- Protection against cross-tenant access.

Salesforce is responsible for operating the underlying platform and implementing the service-level security mechanisms supporting this architecture.

Customers must still configure their own orgs securely.

#### 2.1.4 Multi-Tenancy Security Principles

A secure multi-tenant platform must maintain separation between customer environments.

Important security principles include:

**Tenant Isolation**

A customer must not be able to access another customer's data or application environment without explicit authorization through an approved service relationship.

**Logical Data Separation**

Customer records and application data must remain associated with the appropriate customer environment.

**Access Control**

Users and applications must only access resources permitted by the applicable authorization rules.

**Secure Execution**

Customer application operations must execute within the security boundaries enforced by the platform.

**Resource Governance**

Shared infrastructure must be managed to maintain service reliability and prevent one tenant's resource consumption from unreasonably affecting other tenants.

**Platform Security**

The provider must maintain the security of the underlying infrastructure and shared platform services.

These principles form an important foundation for assessing Salesforce as a cloud service.

### 3. Salesforce Organization Architecture

A Salesforce organization, or org, is the primary logical environment in which a customer configures and operates Salesforce applications.

The org contains the data, users, metadata, security settings and application functionality associated with that environment.

Understanding the org is essential because many Salesforce security controls are configured and enforced within this logical boundary.

#### 3.1.1 Main Components of a Salesforce Org

| Component | Description | Security Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Users | Individuals or service identities accessing Salesforce | Identity and access governance |
| Objects | Structures used to store business records | Object-level security |
| Records | Individual data entries stored in objects | Record-level access control |
| Fields | Attributes associated with objects | Field-level security |
| Profiles | Baseline user permissions and settings | Access configuration |
| Permission Sets | Additional permissions assigned to users | Least-privilege governance |
| Roles | Organizational hierarchy used in record access | Record visibility |
| Sharing Rules | Mechanisms for extending record access | Data access governance |
| Applications | Business functionality exposed to users | Application security |
| Metadata | Configuration and structural definitions | Configuration governance |
| Apex | Salesforce programming language | Custom application security |
| Lightning Web Components | User interface development framework | Front-end application security |
| Flows | Declarative automation capabilities | Business logic and authorization |
| APIs | Interfaces for external application connectivity | Integration security |

#### 3.1.2 Salesforce Org as a Security Boundary

The org is a central administrative and logical boundary for customer-specific configuration and data.

Security assessment activities should therefore begin by identifying the orgs used by the organization.

Typical examples include:

- Production orgs.
- Development environments.
- Testing environments.
- User acceptance testing environments.
- Sandboxes.
- Specialized environments associated with particular Salesforce products or services.

The organization should maintain an inventory of its Salesforce environments, their business purposes, owners, data classifications and integration dependencies.

#### 3.1.3 Production and Non-Production Orgs

Production environments support live business operations.

Non-production environments support development, testing, configuration validation and other controlled activities.

These environments may contain different levels of data sensitivity and operational criticality.

| Environment | Purpose | Security Consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Production | Supports live business operations | Strict access and change controls |
| Development | Supports application development | Developer access and secure coding |
| Testing | Supports functional and technical testing | Controlled test data and restricted access |
| User Acceptance Testing | Supports business validation | Approved business user access |
| Sandbox | Supports isolated development, testing or training activities | Data protection and environment governance |

The organization must not assume that non-production environments are automatically low risk.

A sandbox containing copied customer information may present similar confidentiality risks to production.

### 4. Salesforce Platform Architecture Layers

Salesforce can be understood as a collection of architectural layers that work together to deliver enterprise application functionality.

For cybersecurity and technology governance, these layers provide a useful model for identifying security responsibilities and potential control weaknesses.

#### 4.1.1 Infrastructure Layer

The infrastructure layer provides the underlying computing environment supporting Salesforce services.

It includes the physical and logical infrastructure required to operate the cloud platform.

Examples include:

- Data centre facilities.
- Physical servers.
- Storage infrastructure.
- Network infrastructure.
- Virtualized computing resources.
- Supporting infrastructure services.
- Environmental and physical security capabilities.

Salesforce manages the underlying infrastructure for its SaaS services.

Customers generally do not administer the underlying physical servers or infrastructure operating systems supporting the Salesforce service.

**Cybersecurity relevance:**

The provider must protect the infrastructure against physical threats, infrastructure compromise, unauthorized access and service disruption.

Customers should assess the provider's security assurance, service availability information and shared responsibility commitments.

#### 4.1.2 Platform Services Layer

The platform services layer provides common capabilities used by Salesforce applications.

These capabilities support application execution, data management, automation, identity integration and development.

Examples include:

- Application runtime services.
- Metadata services.
- Database services.
- Workflow and automation capabilities.
- Authentication and authorization services.
- API services.
- Application development capabilities.
- Platform management services.

The platform layer allows customers to configure and extend Salesforce without directly managing the underlying application infrastructure.

**Cybersecurity relevance:**

The platform must enforce appropriate tenant isolation and provide security mechanisms that customers can configure and use.

Customers must understand how platform capabilities operate and configure them according to their requirements.

#### 4.1.3 Application Layer

The application layer provides the business functionality used by customers.

Examples include:

- Sales Cloud.
- Service Cloud.
- Experience Cloud.
- Marketing Cloud.
- Data Cloud.
- Custom Salesforce applications.
- Industry-specific Salesforce solutions.

The application layer supports business processes such as customer relationship management, customer support, sales operations and digital engagement.

**Cybersecurity relevance:**

Security assessments must consider the application's configuration, data access model, business logic, custom development and connected services.

#### 4.1.4 Presentation Layer

The presentation layer provides the interfaces through which users interact with Salesforce applications.

Examples include:

- Salesforce Lightning Experience.
- Salesforce mobile applications.
- Experience Cloud websites and portals.
- Custom Lightning Web Components.
- External applications using Salesforce APIs.

The presentation layer enables users to access business functionality.

However, hiding a user interface element does not necessarily prevent access to the underlying data or operation.

Authorization must be enforced through the appropriate server-side and platform security mechanisms.

**Cybersecurity relevance:**

Testing must validate actual authorization and data access behavior rather than relying only on what is visible in the user interface.

#### 4.1.5 Integration Layer

The integration layer connects Salesforce with external applications and enterprise services.

Examples include:

- REST APIs.
- SOAP APIs.
- Bulk APIs.
- MuleSoft.
- Middleware.
- Enterprise integration platforms.
- External identity providers.
- Event-driven integrations.
- Third-party SaaS applications.

**Cybersecurity relevance:**

The integration layer introduces additional identities, trust relationships, data transfers and attack surfaces.

Security requirements must address authentication, authorization, credential protection, data validation, transport security and monitoring.

### 5. Salesforce Application Runtime and Metadata Architecture

Salesforce uses a metadata-driven architecture that allows customers to configure and extend applications through platform capabilities.

Understanding this architecture is important because application behavior is influenced by both the underlying platform and customer-defined metadata.

#### 5.1.1 Metadata-Driven Architecture

Metadata describes the structure, configuration and behavior of an application.

Examples include:

- Object definitions.
- Field definitions.
- Page layouts.
- Record types.
- Validation rules.
- Profiles.
- Permission sets.
- Sharing settings.
- Flows.
- Apex classes.
- Lightning Web Components.
- Application configurations.

Instead of requiring customers to build every application component from the ground up, Salesforce provides standard platform capabilities that can be configured through metadata.

For example, an organization can create a custom object called `Customer_Verification__c` to manage customer verification information.

The object can include fields such as:

- Verification reference.
- Verification status.
- Verification date.
- Verification provider.
- Review outcome.

The organization can configure access permissions, page layouts, validation rules and automation around the object.

#### 5.1.2 Metadata and Data Separation

Metadata and business data serve different purposes.

| Element             | Metadata                                         | Business Data                               |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------- |
| Purpose             | Defines application structure and behavior       | Stores operational information              |
| Examples            | Object definitions and permission configurations | Customer records and service cases          |
| Security Relevance  | Configuration and access governance              | Confidentiality, integrity and availability |
| Change Management   | Requires controlled configuration changes        | Requires controlled data operations         |
| Backup and Recovery | Metadata recovery and deployment governance      | Data recovery and retention governance      |

A security assessment must evaluate both.

Unauthorized modification of metadata may weaken access controls or alter application behavior.

Unauthorized modification of business data may affect customer information and operational transactions.

#### 5.1.3 Metadata Dependencies

Salesforce application components may depend on other metadata and configuration elements.

For example, a custom application may depend on:

- Custom objects.
- Fields.
- Permission sets.
- Apex classes.
- Flows.
- Lightning Web Components.
- External services.
- Connected applications.

A change to one component may affect the behavior or security of dependent components.

The organization should therefore maintain configuration documentation and controlled deployment procedures.

#### 5.1.4 Metadata Security Risks

Potential risks include:

- Unauthorized changes to permission configurations.
- Modification of sharing rules.
- Insecure application deployments.
- Introduction of vulnerable Apex code.
- Changes to authentication configurations.
- Unauthorized creation of connected applications.
- Inconsistent configurations between environments.

Recommended governance controls include:

- Restrict metadata modification privileges.
- Use approved development and deployment processes.
- Maintain source control where applicable.
- Require peer reviews.
- Perform security testing.
- Maintain deployment evidence.
- Monitor sensitive configuration changes.

### 6. Salesforce Database and Data Architecture

Salesforce provides data management capabilities that allow organizations to create, store, retrieve and process business information.

Its data architecture is based on objects, fields and records, with relationships connecting information across business entities.

Understanding this model is essential for evaluating data protection, access control and information governance.

#### 6.1.1 Objects

Objects represent structures used to store business information.

Salesforce supports standard objects and custom objects.

Standard Objects

Standard objects are provided by Salesforce.

Examples include:

- Account.
- Contact.
- Lead.
- Opportunity.
- Case.
- Campaign.
- User.

Custom Objects

Custom objects are created by customers to support specific business requirements.

For example:

- Customer Verification.
- Service Subscription.
- Product Registration.
- Delivery Request.
- Customer Risk Assessment.

Custom objects are identified by the `__c` suffix in their API names.

#### 6.1.2 Fields

Fields represent individual attributes within an object.

For example, a Contact object may contain:

- First Name.
- Last Name.
- Email.
- Phone.
- Mailing Address.
- Account association.

Fields may contain different data types, including text, numbers, dates, currency and references to other records.

Security professionals must identify sensitive fields and determine which users and applications require access.

#### 6.1.3 Records

Records represent individual entries within an object.

For example, a Contact object may contain a record representing a specific customer.

Each record may contain multiple fields and relationships with other records.

Record-level access controls determine which users can view or modify particular records.

#### 6.1.4 Object Relationships

Salesforce supports relationships between objects to represent business information.

Common relationship types include:

- Lookup relationships.
- Master-detail relationships.
- Hierarchical relationships, for supported use cases.

Example:

An Account may be associated with multiple Contact records.

A customer support Case may be associated with an Account and Contact.

These relationships support enterprise data models and business processes.

The Salesforce data model directly influences how information is protected.

Security assessments should evaluate:

### 7. Salesforce Application Development Architecture

Salesforce supports declarative configuration and programmatic development.

This allows organizations to build custom business functionality while using the underlying Salesforce platform.

The development architecture is relevant to cybersecurity because custom application components introduce additional security responsibilities.

#### 7.1.1 Declarative Development

Declarative development allows administrators and developers to configure application functionality using platform tools rather than writing traditional application code for every requirement.

Examples include:

- Flow Builder.
- Validation Rules.
- Formula Fields.
- Approval Processes.
- Page Layouts.
- Record Types.
- Object Configuration.

Declarative capabilities can reduce development complexity, but they still require security design and testing.

For example, a Flow may perform actions that affect sensitive records. Its execution context, access permissions and data handling must be evaluated.

#### 7.1.2 Programmatic Development

Salesforce supports programmatic development using technologies such as:

- Apex.
- Lightning Web Components.
- Visualforce.
- Salesforce APIs.

Apex

Apex is Salesforce's proprietary programming language used to implement server-side business logic.

It supports activities such as:

- Business process execution.
- Database operations.
- Trigger-based processing.
- Integration callouts.
- Custom application services.

Lightning Web Components

Lightning Web Components (LWC) provide a modern framework for building user interfaces on the Salesforce Platform.

They can support interactive application components and communicate with server-side functionality.

Visualforce

Visualforce is a Salesforce framework for building custom user interfaces using a component-based markup approach.

It remains relevant in some existing Salesforce implementations.

#### 7.1.3 Application Development Security

Custom development must address security throughout the software development lifecycle.

Key requirements include:

- Secure coding standards.
- Authentication and authorization.
- Input validation.
- Secure database operations.
- Protection against injection.
- Secure handling of sensitive data.
- Appropriate execution context.
- Dependency management.
- Code review.
- Security testing.
- Controlled deployment.

A developer must not assume that the Salesforce platform automatically protects every custom application from insecure implementation.

#### 7.1.4 Development Architecture Example

A proposed customer verification application may use the following components:

1. A Lightning Web Component provides the user interface.
2. An Apex service processes the verification request.
3. Salesforce objects store the verification status and reference information.
4. A Flow initiates an approved business process.
5. An API integration communicates with an external verification provider.
6. Security controls restrict access to sensitive verification information.

Each component introduces different security requirements.

The user interface must not be treated as the only enforcement point for access control. Server-side authorization, integration security and data protection must also be addressed.

### 8. Salesforce Database Operations and Platform Limits

Salesforce operates as a shared cloud platform. Its architecture must manage resource consumption, application execution and service reliability across customer environments.

The platform therefore applies technical limits and execution constraints to supported operations.

These limits are important for architecture design, performance management and cybersecurity.

#### 8.1.1 Platform Limits

Salesforce applies various limits to platform operations.

Depending on the service, edition, license and execution context, these may include:

- API request limits.
- Apex governor limits.
- Database query limits.
- Data storage limits.
- File storage limits.
- Concurrent processing constraints.
- Automation execution limits.

The specific limits are not universal and may vary according to the Salesforce product and subscription.

Organizations should consult current product documentation when designing applications or evaluating capacity requirements.

#### 8.1.2 Apex Governor Limits

Apex governor limits are platform-enforced execution limits designed to manage resource consumption.

They help prevent inefficient or excessive application operations from consuming disproportionate shared resources.

Examples of areas subject to limits include:

- Database queries.
- Database operations.
- CPU time.
- Heap size.
- Callouts.
- Execution behavior.

Developers must design Apex code to operate within applicable limits.

Poorly designed applications may encounter execution failures or operational disruption when limits are exceeded.

#### 8.1.3 API Limits

Salesforce APIs allow external applications to access and exchange data with the platform.

API usage may be subject to limits based on the applicable service, license and org configuration.

Organizations should monitor API consumption and design integrations to avoid unnecessary requests.

Security and operational considerations include:

- Unauthorized API usage.
- Excessive data extraction.
- API credential compromise.
- Integration service disruption.
- Unexpected API consumption.
- Failure of dependent business processes.

#### 8.1.4 Resource Consumption and Security Implications

Resource limits are not only a performance concern.

They can also influence availability and business continuity.

For example, an inefficient integration that generates excessive API requests may affect the availability of business processes that depend on Salesforce.

Similarly, poorly designed automation may fail under increased transaction volumes.

The organization should therefore include platform limits and resource dependencies in architecture risk assessments.

### 9. Salesforce Trust Boundaries and Security Domains

A trust boundary defines where security responsibilities, access assumptions or control requirements change.

Salesforce environments contain multiple trust boundaries because users, applications, providers and enterprise systems interact through different interfaces.

Identifying these boundaries is essential for security architecture and threat modeling.

#### 9.1.1 Salesforce Provider Boundary

The Salesforce provider boundary covers the infrastructure and platform services operated by Salesforce.

It includes responsibilities associated with:

- Underlying infrastructure security.
- Physical data centre security.
- Platform operations.
- Shared service security.
- Provider-managed service availability.
- Platform maintenance and service updates.

The organization must understand the provider's commitments and service-specific responsibilities.

#### 9.1.2 Customer Organization Boundary

The customer organization boundary includes the Salesforce org and the configurations, data and applications managed by the customer.

It includes:

- User accounts.
- Permission assignments.
- Business data.
- Custom application components.
- Sharing configuration.
- Integration settings.
- Customer-defined automation.
- Security policies and procedures.

The organization is responsible for configuring these components according to its security requirements.

#### 9.1.3 External Identity Boundary

An enterprise identity provider may authenticate users before they access Salesforce.

This introduces a trust relationship between Salesforce and the identity provider.

Security considerations include:

- Identity federation.
- Authentication assurance.
- Token security.
- Session management.
- Identity lifecycle management.
- Privileged account protection.

A compromise of the identity provider may affect access to multiple enterprise services, including Salesforce.

#### 9.1.4 Integration Boundary

Salesforce integrations establish trust relationships with external applications and middleware.

Examples include:

- MuleSoft.
- SAP S/4HANA.
- E-commerce platforms.
- Payment services.
- Identity verification providers.
- External SaaS applications.

Each integration requires appropriate authentication, authorization, credential protection and data exchange controls.

#### 9.1.5 User and Endpoint Boundary

Users access Salesforce through browsers, mobile devices and other supported clients.

These endpoints may be managed by the organization or operated by external parties.

Security considerations include:

- Device security.
- Endpoint protection.
- Browser security.
- Authentication.
- Session protection.
- Network connectivity.
- User awareness.

Salesforce's multi-tenant model creates a shared responsibility arrangement between the service provider and the customer organization.

Salesforce responsibilities generally include the provider-managed infrastructure and platform security domains.

- Physical infrastructure security.
- Data centre security.
- Underlying infrastructure management.
- Platform operations.
- Provider-managed service availability.
- Security of shared platform services.
- Platform maintenance.
- Provider-side vulnerability and security management.

The precise responsibilities should be validated against the applicable Salesforce service documentation and contractual commitments.

- User identity governance.
- Authentication configuration.
- User permissions.
- Record-sharing configuration.
- Customer data classification.
- Custom application security.
- API and integration security.
- Third-party application governance.
- Security monitoring of available customer-side events.
- Incident response coordination.
- Regulatory and contractual compliance.

Examples include:

- Middleware providers.
- Identity providers.
- Payment service providers.
- External data processors.
- AppExchange application providers.
- External AI service providers.
- Managed security service providers.

### 12. Architecture Dependencies, Limitations and Security Considerations

Salesforce's architecture introduces several dependencies that must be considered during technology risk assessments.

#### 12.1.1 Platform Dependency

Customers depend on Salesforce to operate the underlying cloud service.

Potential considerations include:

- Service availability.
- Platform maintenance.
- Product functionality.
- Service limitations.
- Provider security commitments.
- Regional service dependencies.

The organization should assess these dependencies against business continuity and operational resilience requirements.

#### 12.1.2 Configuration Dependency

The security posture of a Salesforce org depends significantly on customer configuration.

Examples include:

- Authentication settings.
- Permission assignments.
- Sharing configuration.
- Data access controls.
- Connected applications.
- Integration settings.
- Custom application security.

Incorrect configuration can create vulnerabilities even when the underlying platform operates as intended.

#### 12.1.3 Integration Dependency

Enterprise processes may depend on the availability and security of multiple connected applications.

A Salesforce environment may be operational while a critical external integration is unavailable.

For example, a customer service representative may be able to access Salesforce but unable to retrieve current billing or order information because an integration service is unavailable.

The organization should therefore assess the complete business service rather than Salesforce in isolation.

#### 12.1.4 Licensing and Feature Dependency

Salesforce products and security capabilities may depend on subscription, edition, add-on licensing and configuration.

Organizations should verify:

- Product availability.
- Feature entitlements.
- Event Monitoring access.
- Encryption capabilities.
- Data storage requirements.
- API usage entitlements.
- Integration capabilities.
- Retention options.

Security architecture must not assume that every Salesforce org includes every advanced security capability.

#### 12.1.5 Multi-Tenant Architecture Limitations

The multi-tenant model provides operational and scalability advantages, but it also means customers have limited control over the underlying infrastructure.

Customers generally cannot:

- Administer the physical servers supporting the service.
- Directly manage the underlying operating systems.
- Independently modify Salesforce-managed infrastructure.
- Implement arbitrary infrastructure-level security controls.
- Control the provider's internal platform architecture.

Customers must instead use the security capabilities, configuration options and integration mechanisms supported by Salesforce.

This affects how security assessments, penetration testing, vulnerability management and incident response should be designed.

## Part 2 - Threats, Vulnerabilities and Risk Assessment

### 1. Security Risk Overview

Salesforce's multi-tenant architecture introduces a distinct set of cybersecurity risks because multiple customer organizations operate on shared underlying platform infrastructure while maintaining logically separated environments.

Although Salesforce manages the underlying infrastructure and platform security, customers remain responsible for protecting their own Salesforce organizations, data, identities, configurations, custom applications and integrations.

A Salesforce architecture risk assessment must therefore examine two interconnected security domains:

1. **Provider-managed security:** Risks associated with the underlying infrastructure, shared platform services, tenant isolation and Salesforce service operations.
2. **Customer-managed security:** Risks associated with org configuration, user access, application development, data governance, integrations and operational security.

The distinction is important because customers do not have direct administrative control over Salesforce's underlying infrastructure. Their security assessment must focus on the provider's assurance commitments and the security controls available within their own environments.

#### 1.1.1 Primary Risk Categories

| Risk Category | Description | Potential Business Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Multi-Tenant Isolation | Failure of logical separation between customer environments | Unauthorized cross-tenant data exposure |
| Platform Security | Weaknesses affecting shared platform services | Platform compromise or service disruption |
| Data Isolation | Inadequate protection of customer-specific data | Unauthorized disclosure or modification |
| Identity and Access | Compromised accounts or excessive permissions | Unauthorized access to Salesforce resources |
| Configuration Security | Incorrect org-level security settings | Exposure of sensitive information |
| Metadata Security | Unauthorized modification of application metadata | Security control bypass or application manipulation |
| Application Runtime | Vulnerabilities in custom application execution | Unauthorized operations or application compromise |
| Database and Data Access | Weaknesses in object, field or record-level permissions | Customer information exposure |
| Resource Consumption | Excessive platform resource usage or inefficient processing | Application degradation or service disruption |
| API and Integration | Insecure external connectivity | Unauthorized data exchange or integration compromise |
| Environment Management | Weaknesses in sandbox and production governance | Data exposure or unauthorized production changes |
| Provider Dependency | Dependence on Salesforce infrastructure and service availability | Business interruption |
| Security Monitoring | Incomplete visibility into platform and customer activities | Delayed incident detection |

#### 1.1.2 Multi-Tenant Risk Characteristics

Multi-tenant architecture creates specific risk considerations that differ from traditional dedicated infrastructure environments.

**Shared Infrastructure Dependency**

Customers depend on the security and availability of the underlying platform operated by Salesforce.

**Logical Isolation Dependency**

Customer data and application environments depend on the provider's tenant isolation mechanisms.

**Limited Infrastructure Visibility**

Customers generally cannot directly inspect or administer the underlying infrastructure supporting Salesforce.

**Configuration Responsibility**

Customers must correctly configure the security mechanisms available within their Salesforce organizations.

**Shared Resource Constraints**

Applications operate within platform-enforced limits and resource management mechanisms.

**Provider Dependency**

Customers depend on Salesforce for platform maintenance, infrastructure security and service operations.

These characteristics must be reflected in the organization's cloud security risk assessment and third-party risk management processes.

### 2. Threat Landscape

The Salesforce threat landscape includes threats targeting the underlying cloud service, customer organizations, application components, identities and connected enterprise systems.

Threat actors may exploit weaknesses in customer configurations or attempt to compromise accounts and integrations that provide access to Salesforce.

#### 2.1.1 External Attackers

External attackers may target Salesforce environments to steal customer information, compromise business accounts, manipulate records or disrupt enterprise operations.

Potential attack techniques include:

- Credential phishing.
- Password spraying.
- Credential stuffing.
- Session hijacking.
- OAuth token theft.
- API abuse.
- Exploitation of vulnerable custom applications.
- Unauthorized data extraction.
- Exploitation of vulnerable external integrations.

An external attacker does not necessarily need to compromise Salesforce's underlying infrastructure to affect a customer's Salesforce environment.

Compromising a customer account, integration identity or connected application may provide sufficient access to cause significant damage.

#### 2.1.2 Malicious Insiders

Employees, administrators, developers and contractors may misuse legitimate access.

Potential activities include:

- Accessing customer records without business justification.
- Exporting confidential information.
- Modifying customer records.
- Changing security configurations.
- Creating unauthorized connected applications.
- Disclosing sensitive information.
- Retaining access after employment or contract termination.

Insider threats are particularly relevant in multi-tenant SaaS environments because customers rely heavily on logical access controls rather than direct infrastructure administration.

#### 2.1.3 Compromised Administrators

Salesforce administrators have access to configuration capabilities that may affect the security of the entire customer organization.

An attacker who compromises a privileged administrator account may attempt to:

- Create unauthorized users.
- Assign excessive permissions.
- Modify sharing settings.
- Change authentication configurations.
- Create connected applications.
- Modify automation.
- Deploy malicious metadata.
- Access sensitive customer records.

The potential impact depends on the administrator's assigned privileges and the security controls protecting the account.

#### 2.1.4 Third-Party and Supply-Chain Threats

Salesforce environments may depend on external applications, middleware, identity providers and service providers.

A compromise of one of these services may create indirect exposure.

Examples include:

- Compromise of an AppExchange application provider.
- Compromise of a MuleSoft or middleware environment.
- Theft of integration credentials.
- Compromise of an enterprise identity provider.
- Malicious modification of an external application.
- Vulnerability in a third-party package.
- Unauthorized use of external AI services.

Third-party security must therefore be assessed as part of the overall Salesforce architecture.

#### 2.1.5 Platform-Level Threats

Platform-level threats concern the underlying shared service environment.

Potential threat scenarios include:

- Exploitation of a vulnerability in shared platform services.
- Unauthorized access to underlying infrastructure.
- Failure of tenant isolation mechanisms.
- Compromise of provider-managed infrastructure.
- Disruption of shared platform services.
- Exploitation of resource management weaknesses.

These scenarios are primarily within Salesforce's infrastructure and platform security responsibilities.

Customers should evaluate the provider's security assurance, service commitments and incident communication arrangements rather than assume they can independently test or remediate the underlying infrastructure.

### 3. Multi-Tenant Architecture Attack Surface Analysis

The attack surface of Salesforce extends beyond the customer-facing application interface.

It includes the customer org, user identities, metadata, application runtime, data access mechanisms, APIs and external dependencies.

#### 3.1.1 Tenant Isolation Attack Surface

Tenant isolation is fundamental to multi-tenant architecture.

Each customer org must remain logically separated from other customer environments.

Potential attack surfaces include:

- Tenant identification mechanisms.
- Platform authorization services.
- Shared application runtime services.
- Database access mechanisms.
- Internal platform service interfaces.
- Shared infrastructure components.

A failure in tenant isolation could potentially expose one customer's information to another customer.

However, customers generally cannot directly inspect the provider's internal tenant isolation implementation.

The appropriate customer-side assessment should evaluate Salesforce's documented security architecture, independent assurance information and contractual commitments.

#### 3.1.2 Salesforce Org Attack Surface

The customer org contains the configurations and application components that customers administer.

Potential attack surfaces include:

- User accounts.
- Administrator accounts.
- Profiles.
- Permission sets.
- Permission set groups.
- Sharing rules.
- Custom objects.
- Custom fields.
- Flows.
- Apex classes.
- Lightning Web Components.
- Connected applications.
- API configurations.

Incorrect configuration or insecure development can expose customer data and business processes.

#### 3.1.3 Metadata Attack Surface

Metadata defines the structure and behavior of Salesforce applications.

Potential attack surfaces include:

- Object definitions.
- Field definitions.
- Permission configurations.
- Sharing settings.
- Validation rules.
- Apex classes.
- Flows.
- Lightning Web Components.
- Application configuration.
- Deployment packages.

Unauthorized metadata changes may weaken security controls or alter application behavior.

For example, an unauthorized modification to a permission set may provide additional access to sensitive customer records.

#### 3.1.4 Application Runtime Attack Surface

The application runtime executes customer-defined business logic and application operations.

Potential attack surfaces include:

- Apex execution.
- Database operations.
- Flow execution.
- Trigger execution.
- External service callouts.
- Custom application interfaces.
- Business process automation.

Security weaknesses may arise from incorrect authorization checks, insecure input handling or poorly designed business logic.

#### 3.1.5 Database and Data Access Attack Surface

Salesforce data is accessed through platform interfaces, application functionality and APIs.

Potential attack surfaces include:

- Object permissions.
- Field-level security.
- Record-level sharing.
- SOQL and SOSL queries.
- Data import and export.
- Reports and dashboards.
- Files and attachments.
- Bulk data operations.

The organization must validate that users and applications can access only the information required for their legitimate business activities.

#### 3.1.6 API and Integration Attack Surface

External applications interact with Salesforce through supported integration mechanisms.

Potential attack surfaces include:

- REST APIs.
- SOAP APIs.
- Bulk APIs.
- OAuth authorization.
- Connected applications.
- Integration identities.
- Middleware services.
- External service callouts.
- Event-based integrations.

Weaknesses in these interfaces may allow unauthorized access, data manipulation or excessive data extraction.

#### 3.1.7 Non-Production Environment Attack Surface

Development and testing environments may introduce additional exposure.

Potential weaknesses include:

- Production-derived customer data in sandboxes.
- Excessive developer permissions.
- Unrestricted sandbox access.
- Insecure test integrations.
- Weak credential management.
- Inconsistent security configurations.
- Uncontrolled deployment processes.

Non-production environments must be included in the Salesforce security assessment because they may contain sensitive data and application components.

### 4. Multi-Tenant Isolation Vulnerabilities

Tenant isolation is one of the most important security properties of a multi-tenant SaaS architecture.

It ensures that customer environments remain logically separated even though they use shared underlying infrastructure.

#### 4.1.1 Inadequate Tenant Isolation

A tenant isolation weakness occurs when a customer environment can access information or resources belonging to another customer without authorization.

Potential consequences include:

- Cross-customer data exposure.
- Unauthorized access to application resources.
- Disclosure of confidential business information.
- Regulatory and contractual consequences.
- Loss of customer trust.

The underlying tenant isolation mechanisms are managed by Salesforce.

Customer organizations should assess provider assurance and monitor relevant security communications.

#### 4.1.2 Cross-Tenant Data Exposure

Cross-tenant data exposure refers to unauthorized disclosure of information between different customer environments.

Potential causes at the provider level could include:

- Defects in shared platform authorization mechanisms.
- Application isolation failures.
- Database access control failures.
- Incorrect platform service implementation.
- Security vulnerabilities in shared services.

This is fundamentally different from a customer misconfiguring its own sharing rules.

A customer sharing configuration error normally exposes information within that customer's own org. It does not, by itself, establish that another customer's data is accessible.

#### 4.1.3 Shared Resource Abuse

Because multiple customers use shared infrastructure, the provider must manage resource consumption and service performance.

Potential risk scenarios include:

- Excessive resource consumption.
- Inefficient application processing.
- Abnormal transaction volumes.
- Platform resource exhaustion.
- Service degradation.

Salesforce applies platform limits and resource management mechanisms to support service stability.

Customers must design applications and integrations to operate within applicable platform limits.

#### 4.1.4 Tenant Isolation Risk Assessment

| Element | Description |
|---|---|
| Asset | Salesforce shared platform and customer data |
| Threat | External attacker or platform-level threat actor |
| Vulnerability | Potential weakness in tenant isolation mechanisms |
| Attack Vector | Exploitation of a shared platform service |
| Cause | Provider-side platform security defect or control failure |
| Potential Consequence | Unauthorized cross-tenant information access |
| Customer Control | Provider assurance review and security governance |
| Provider Control | Platform isolation and shared service security |
| Business Impact | Confidentiality breach, regulatory exposure and reputational damage |

The scenario represents a provider-level risk. Customers cannot independently verify internal tenant isolation controls through normal org administration.

### 5. Customer Organization Configuration Vulnerabilities

Although Salesforce manages the underlying platform, the customer organization remains responsible for configuring its own environment securely.

Incorrect org configuration can create vulnerabilities even when the provider-managed infrastructure operates correctly.

#### 5.1.1 Excessive User Permissions

Users may receive permissions beyond their business requirements.

Potential causes include:

- Overly broad profiles.
- Excessive permission sets.
- Inappropriate permission set groups.
- Uncontrolled administrative access.
- Lack of periodic access reviews.

Potential consequences include:

- Unauthorized customer record access.
- Data modification.
- Privilege abuse.
- Increased impact of account compromise.

#### 5.1.2 Incorrect Organization-Wide Defaults

Organization-wide defaults establish baseline record access.

An overly permissive configuration may expose records to more users than intended.

Potential weaknesses include:

- Public Read/Write access without sufficient justification.
- Public Read Only access for sensitive records.
- Inadequate object-specific sharing design.
- Incorrect relationship assumptions.
- Inadequate review of access inheritance.

The organization should evaluate the actual access behavior of each relevant object.

#### 5.1.3 Inadequate Field-Level Security

Field-level security controls whether users can view or edit individual fields.

Potential weaknesses include:

- Sensitive fields visible to unnecessary users.
- Excessive edit permissions.
- Inconsistent permission assignments.
- Missing access reviews.
- Inadequate separation of sensitive business information.

For example, a customer service representative may require access to a customer's contact information but not necessarily to sensitive financial or identity verification fields.

#### 5.1.4 Inadequate Sharing Configuration

Sharing rules and other record-sharing mechanisms can extend access beyond the baseline permissions.

Potential weaknesses include:

- Overly broad sharing rules.
- Incorrect role hierarchy design.
- Excessive manual sharing.
- Unreviewed sharing exceptions.
- Inappropriate access granted through groups.

The combined effect of profiles, permission sets, role hierarchy, sharing rules and other access mechanisms must be evaluated.

#### 5.1.5 Insecure Connected Application Configuration

Connected applications may request permissions that allow external applications to interact with Salesforce.

Potential weaknesses include:

- Excessive OAuth scopes.
- Unapproved connected applications.
- Inadequate application ownership.
- Weak credential management.
- Insufficient authorization review.
- Unmonitored application activity.

An application with excessive permissions may create a significant attack path if its credentials or provider are compromised.

### 6. Metadata and Application Runtime Vulnerabilities

Salesforce metadata and runtime services enable organizations to customize business applications.

However, these capabilities introduce security responsibilities for administrators and developers.

#### 6.1.1 Unauthorized Metadata Modification

Metadata changes can affect application functionality and security configuration.

Potential weaknesses include:

- Excessive metadata modification privileges.
- Uncontrolled deployment access.
- Missing approval workflows.
- Inadequate source control.
- Insufficient deployment testing.
- Unmonitored production changes.

An attacker with sufficient privileges may modify application configuration to weaken security restrictions.

#### 6.1.2 Insecure Apex Execution

Apex is used to implement server-side business logic.

Poorly designed Apex code may introduce vulnerabilities such as:

- Missing authorization checks.
- Insecure SOQL construction.
- Inadequate input validation.
- Excessive execution privileges.
- Improper handling of sensitive information.
- Insecure external callouts.

Apex execution behavior must be assessed according to its security context.

Developers must evaluate whether record sharing, object permissions and field-level security are appropriately enforced.

#### 6.1.3 Insecure Flow Configuration

Salesforce Flow provides declarative automation capabilities.

Flows can perform operations that affect records, trigger business processes and interact with other platform components.

Potential weaknesses include:

- Inappropriate execution context.
- Excessive permissions.
- Inadequate input validation.
- Unrestricted record modification.
- Insecure data handling.
- Missing approval controls.
- Incorrect automation logic.

A Flow that performs operations using permissions broader than those of the initiating user may introduce unintended access paths.

#### 6.1.4 Lightning Web Component Vulnerabilities

Lightning Web Components provide a framework for building Salesforce user interfaces.

Potential weaknesses include:

- Inadequate server-side authorization.
- Improper handling of user-controlled input.
- Insecure communication with Apex services.
- Exposure of sensitive information in client-side logic.
- Inadequate validation of application responses.
- Incorrect assumptions about UI-based access restrictions.

Developers must ensure that security controls are enforced beyond the presentation layer.

### 7. Database and Data Access Vulnerabilities

Salesforce data security depends on the correct implementation of object permissions, field-level security, record-level access and application authorization.

Weaknesses in these mechanisms can expose sensitive business information.

#### 7.1.1 Object-Level Security Weaknesses

Object-level permissions determine whether users can perform operations such as:

- Read.
- Create.
- Edit.
- Delete.
- View All.
- Modify All.

Potential weaknesses include:

- Unnecessary object access.
- Excessive modification privileges.
- Inappropriate View All permissions.
- Inappropriate Modify All permissions.
- Inadequate separation of duties.

A user with broad object permissions may have access to more business information than required.

#### 7.1.2 Field-Level Security Weaknesses

Field-level security controls access to individual fields.

Potential weaknesses include:

- Sensitive fields exposed to general business users.
- Unnecessary field modification privileges.
- Inconsistent access between profiles and permission sets.
- Inadequate validation of sensitive field access.

The organization must classify sensitive fields and define approved access requirements.

#### 7.1.3 Record-Level Security Weaknesses

Record-level access determines which specific records users can access.

Potential weaknesses include:

- Excessive organization-wide defaults.
- Incorrect sharing rules.
- Inadequate role hierarchy design.
- Excessive manual sharing.
- Uncontrolled sharing groups.

A user may have legitimate access to an object but should not necessarily have access to every record within that object.

#### 7.1.4 Insecure Query and Data Retrieval Logic

Custom applications may retrieve information using SOQL or SOSL.

Potential weaknesses include:

- Dynamic query construction using untrusted input.
- Missing authorization checks.
- Inadequate enforcement of object permissions.
- Inadequate field-level security enforcement.
- Excessive data retrieval.
- Incorrect sharing behavior.

Developers must use secure query construction and enforce the appropriate access controls.

#### 7.1.5 Data Export and Exfiltration

Salesforce provides functionality for reporting, data extraction and integration.

Potential weaknesses include:

- Excessive export privileges.
- Unrestricted report access.
- Unmonitored bulk data operations.
- Inadequate restrictions on sensitive files.
- Weak monitoring of API-based extraction.
- Inadequate data loss prevention.

An attacker may exploit legitimate functionality to extract large volumes of information without exploiting a software vulnerability.

### 8. API and Integration Vulnerabilities

Salesforce APIs connect the platform with internal and external applications.

These integrations create trust relationships that may extend the Salesforce attack surface beyond the customer org.

#### 8.1.1 Weak API Authentication

API integrations require appropriate authentication.

Potential weaknesses include:

- Inadequately protected credentials.
- Weak OAuth configuration.
- Long-lived or improperly managed tokens.
- Unapproved authentication methods.
- Poor credential rotation practices.
- Uncontrolled application authorization.

A compromised API credential may allow an attacker to perform operations using the privileges assigned to the associated identity.

Potential causes include:

- Reusing administrator accounts.
- Granting broad object permissions.
- Unnecessary data access.
- Inadequate integration requirements.
- Missing periodic access reviews.

An integration identity should receive only the permissions necessary to perform its defined business functions.

#### 8.1.3 Insecure Middleware

Middleware platforms such as MuleSoft may process and exchange Salesforce information.

- Exposed credentials.
- Inadequate access control.
- Insecure application configuration.
- Vulnerable middleware components.
- Insufficient logging.
- Insecure data transformation.
- Unprotected integration endpoints.

#### 8.1.4 Insecure External Service Callouts

Potential weaknesses include:

- Inadequate authentication.
- Insecure endpoint configuration.
- Insufficient response validation.
- Excessive permissions.
- Inadequate error handling.
- Exposure of sensitive information.
- Weak secrets management.

#### 8.1.5 Integration Failure and Data Integrity Risks

Potential consequences include:

- Incorrect customer information.
- Duplicate records.
- Incomplete transactions.
- Incorrect order status.
- Unauthorized business process execution.
- Inconsistent information across systems.

For example, an insecure integration between Salesforce and SAP S/4HANA could allow unauthorized modification of order-related information.

### 10. Inherent and Residual Risk Assessment

Inherent and residual risk analysis helps determine the level of Salesforce architecture risk before and after considering existing controls.

#### 10.1.1 Inherent Risk

Inherent risk represents the level of risk before considering the effect of existing security controls.

For example:

A Salesforce production org processes a large volume of customer information and connects to multiple enterprise applications.

The potential impact of unauthorized access or data manipulation may be significant.

The inherent risk may therefore be high or critical depending on the organization's risk assessment methodology.

#### 10.1.2 Existing Control Assessment

The organization should identify controls that address the specific risk scenario.

Examples include:

- Salesforce tenant isolation mechanisms.
- Provider security assurance.
- Strong authentication.
- Least-privilege access.
- Object and field-level security.
- Record-sharing controls.
- Secure application development.
- API security.
- SIEM monitoring.
- Incident response procedures.
- Configuration governance.

The assessment must determine whether each control is appropriately designed, implemented and operating effectively.

#### 10.1.3 Residual Risk

Residual risk is the exposure remaining after considering the effectiveness of existing controls.

Example:

| Assessment Element | Example |
|---|---|
| Risk Scenario | Unauthorized access to sensitive Salesforce records |
| Inherent Likelihood | 4 - Likely |
| Inherent Impact | 5 - Critical |
| Inherent Risk Score | 20 - Critical |
| Existing Controls | Least privilege, field-level security and access reviews |
| Control Effectiveness | Partially effective |
| Control Gap | Incomplete review of permission assignments |
| Residual Likelihood | 3 - Possible |
| Residual Impact | 5 - Critical |
| Residual Risk Score | 15 - High |

The example demonstrates that existing controls may reduce the likelihood of an incident without reducing the potential business impact.

#### 10.1.4 Provider Risk and Customer Risk

A critical distinction must be maintained between risks managed primarily by Salesforce and risks managed by the customer organization.

| Risk | Primary Risk Management Responsibility | Customer Assessment Approach |
|---|---|---|
| Physical infrastructure compromise | Salesforce | Review provider assurance and security commitments |

| Salesforce service availability | Salesforce, subject to service commitments | Evaluate business continuity dependencies |
| Integration availability | Customer and integration provider | Assess end-to-end service resilience |

The customer should not assign a provider-managed infrastructure risk to its own technical team as if it had direct control over the underlying infrastructure.

Instead, the customer should manage its dependency through vendor risk management, assurance review, contractual governance and business continuity planning.

### 11. Risk Scoring and Prioritization

Risk scoring provides a consistent method for evaluating and prioritizing Salesforce platform architecture risks.

A simple model uses likelihood and impact.

#### 11.1.1 Likelihood Scale

| Rating | Level | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Rare | Highly unlikely under current conditions |
| 2 | Unlikely | Possible but not expected to occur frequently |
| 3 | Possible | Could occur under realistic circumstances |
| 4 | Likely | Expected to occur in many circumstances |
| 5 | Almost Certain | Expected to occur frequently or repeatedly |

Factors influencing likelihood include:

- Threat actor capability.
- Exposure of the affected component.
- Vulnerability severity.
- Existing controls.
- Application complexity.
- Integration dependencies.
- Historical incidents.
- Provider assurance information.
- Operational control maturity.

#### 11.1.2 Impact Scale

| Rating | Level | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Insignificant | Minimal business consequences |
| 2 | Minor | Limited operational or information impact |
| 3 | Moderate | Noticeable operational or financial impact |
| 4 | Major | Significant business disruption or data exposure |
| 5 | Critical | Severe business, regulatory, financial or reputational consequences |

Impact should consider:

- Confidentiality.
- Integrity.
- Availability.
- Financial exposure.
- Regulatory obligations.
- Customer impact.
- Operational disruption.
- Reputational consequences.

#### 11.1.3 Risk Calculation

**Risk Score = Likelihood × Impact**

The resulting score ranges from 1 to 25.

| Score | Risk Level | Treatment Priority |
|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | Low | Maintain controls and monitor |
| 5–9 | Medium | Evaluate improvements |
| 10–16 | High | Prioritize remediation |
| 17–25 | Critical | Immediate management attention |

The organization should formally approve the scoring methodology and thresholds.

This is a proposed risk scoring model, not a Salesforce-defined standard.

## Part 3 - Security Controls, Mitigation and Implementation

### 1. Security Control Framework

Salesforce platform security requires a layered control framework that addresses the risks associated with multi-tenant architecture, customer organization configuration, application execution, data access, integrations and operational dependencies.

The framework must distinguish between controls implemented by Salesforce and controls implemented by the customer organization.

This distinction is fundamental to cloud security governance. Customers cannot directly configure the provider's underlying infrastructure, but they can assess provider assurance and implement controls over their own Salesforce environments.

The proposed framework is based on five security domains:

1. **Provider and platform assurance:** Evaluate Salesforce's security responsibilities and service commitments.
2. **Tenant and organization security:** Protect the customer org through identity, authorization and configuration controls.
3. **Application and data security:** Secure metadata, custom applications, database access and business logic.
4. **Integration and ecosystem security:** Protect APIs, middleware, connected applications and third-party dependencies.
5. **Security operations and governance:** Monitor security activities, manage incidents and demonstrate control effectiveness.

#### 1.1.1 Security Control Objectives

| Security Domain | Control Objective | Expected Security Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Multi-Tenant Security | Obtain assurance over tenant isolation and shared platform security | Reduce provider dependency risk |
| Identity Security | Authenticate users and protect privileged accounts | Prevent unauthorized access |
| Authorization | Enforce least-privilege permissions | Restrict access to authorized resources |
| Configuration Security | Maintain approved security configurations | Prevent security misconfiguration |
| Metadata Security | Protect application metadata and deployment activities | Prevent unauthorized application changes |
| Application Security | Secure Apex, Flows and Lightning components | Reduce custom application vulnerabilities |
| Data Security | Protect customer information | Preserve confidentiality and integrity |
| API Security | Protect external interfaces and integration identities | Prevent unauthorized data exchange |
| Availability | Manage platform and integration dependencies | Reduce business disruption |
| Monitoring | Detect suspicious activity | Improve threat detection |
| Governance | Establish ownership and accountability | Maintain security assurance |

#### 1.1.2 Defense-in-Depth Model

A defense-in-depth model applies multiple layers of security controls.

For example, protecting customer information should not depend exclusively on Salesforce record-sharing settings.

The organization should combine:

- Strong authentication.
- Least-privilege authorization.
- Object and field-level security.
- Record-level access controls.
- Secure application development.
- API security.
- Data protection.
- Security monitoring.
- Incident response.
- Periodic control testing.

If one control fails, additional security layers should reduce the likelihood or impact of unauthorized access.

### 2. Preventive Security Controls

Preventive controls reduce the likelihood of security incidents by restricting unauthorized activities and protecting the Salesforce environment.

#### 2.1.1 Provider Security Assurance Controls

Because Salesforce manages the underlying multi-tenant infrastructure, customers should establish a formal process for evaluating provider security assurance.

Recommended activities include:

- Review Salesforce security and compliance documentation.
- Evaluate available independent assurance reports.
- Review relevant service commitments.
- Understand provider and customer responsibilities.
- Evaluate the provider's security incident communication arrangements.
- Review service availability and resilience information.
- Assess material changes to Salesforce services.
- Maintain evidence of periodic provider reviews.

The customer should not attempt to treat provider-managed infrastructure as if it were directly administered by its own infrastructure team.

Instead, provider risk should be managed through vendor governance, contractual oversight and independent assurance.

#### 2.1.2 Tenant Isolation Assurance

Tenant isolation is a provider-managed security responsibility.

The customer should evaluate the provider's documented security commitments and assurance evidence concerning logical separation of customer environments.

Recommended governance controls include:

1. Identify the Salesforce services used.
2. Review the applicable security documentation.
3. Obtain relevant independent assurance information.
4. Identify any service-specific exceptions or limitations.
5. Evaluate the impact of a hypothetical isolation failure.
6. Document the provider dependency in the risk register.
7. Assign an accountable vendor or technology risk owner.
8. Reassess provider assurance periodically.

The organization should not claim that it has independently validated Salesforce's internal tenant isolation mechanisms unless it has appropriate evidence and authorization.

#### 2.1.3 Identity and Authentication Controls

Identity security protects access to the Salesforce org.

Recommended controls include:

- Enforce required multi-factor authentication.
- Integrate Salesforce with the approved enterprise identity provider where appropriate.
- Apply strong authentication policies.
- Use phishing-resistant authentication where feasible.
- Restrict privileged access.
- Implement user lifecycle management.
- Disable unnecessary accounts.
- Review authentication and session policies.
- Monitor suspicious authentication activities.
- Establish emergency access procedures.

The organization must validate the authentication behavior of all relevant access paths, including supported API and connected application authentication mechanisms.

#### 2.1.4 Authorization and Least-Privilege Controls

Salesforce provides several mechanisms for managing user access.

| Salesforce Mechanism | Security Purpose | Recommended Control |
|---|---|---|
| Profiles | Establish baseline permissions and settings | Minimize broad permissions |
| Permission Sets | Grant additional access | Assign only approved permissions |
| Permission Set Groups | Combine permission sets | Review combined access |
| Organization-Wide Defaults | Establish baseline record access | Apply restrictive defaults where appropriate |
| Role Hierarchy | Supports record access based on hierarchy | Align with business requirements |
| Sharing Rules | Extend record access | Approve and review sharing rules |
| Manual Sharing | Provides specific record access | Control and monitor exceptions |
| Object Permissions | Control object operations | Apply least privilege |
| Field-Level Security | Restrict individual fields | Protect sensitive attributes |
| Record-Level Security | Control access to specific records | Validate actual record visibility |

Implementation practices should include:

1. Establish an approved business role model.
2. Define the objects and records required by each role.
3. Identify sensitive fields.
4. Design appropriate organization-wide defaults.
5. Review role hierarchy and sharing rules.
6. Use permission sets and permission set groups appropriately.
7. Restrict administrative permissions.
8. Validate access using representative accounts.
9. Perform periodic access reviews.
10. Retain approval and testing evidence.

The organization must evaluate the combined effect of Salesforce access mechanisms rather than treating each permission setting independently.

#### 2.1.5 Privileged Access Management

Salesforce administrators and other privileged users can modify configurations that affect the entire customer org.

Recommended controls include:

- Dedicated administrative identities where appropriate.
- Strong authentication.
- Restricted administrator permissions.
- Formal approval of privileged access.
- Separation of duties.
- Periodic privileged access certification.
- Monitoring of administrative activities.
- Controlled emergency access.
- Prompt removal of unnecessary privileges.

Where an enterprise PAM platform is available, Salesforce administrative access should be assessed for integration with the organization's privileged identity management model.

Recommended controls include:

- Restrict metadata modification privileges.
- Use approved development environments.
- Maintain version control.
- Require peer review.
- Apply change approval procedures.
- Test changes before production deployment.
- Maintain configuration baselines.
- Monitor sensitive configuration changes.
- Retain deployment records.
- Establish rollback procedures.

- Profiles.

- Sharing rules.
- Authentication settings.
- Connected applications.
- Apex classes.
- Flows.
- Custom objects.
- Field definitions.
- Application configurations.

#### 2.1.7 Application Runtime Security

- Secure Apex coding standards.
- Appropriate sharing behavior.
- Explicit authorization checks.
- Object and field permission enforcement.
- Secure SOQL and SOSL construction.
- Input validation.
- Secure external callouts.
- Appropriate Flow execution context.
- Secure Lightning Web Component development.
- Peer code review.
- Dependency assessment.
- Security testing before deployment.

#### 2.1.8 Database and Data Access Controls

- Record-level security.
- Restricted report access.
- Controlled data exports.
- File and attachment access restrictions.
- Data minimization.
- Appropriate encryption.
- Retention and deletion governance.

#### 2.1.9 API and Integration Security

- Approved authentication mechanisms.
- Least-privilege OAuth scopes.
- Dedicated integration identities.
- Secure credential storage.
- Credential rotation and revocation procedures.
- API authorization.
- Input and response validation.
- Secure middleware configuration.
- API usage monitoring.
- Appropriate error handling.
- Integration ownership.
- Periodic integration access reviews.

#### 2.1.10 Non-Production Environment Security

- Restrict sandbox access.
- Protect production-derived information.

- Apply appropriate authentication.
- Restrict integration connectivity.
- Separate development and production privileges.
- Review sandbox permissions.

- Remove unused environments.

### 4. Corrective and Recovery Controls

Corrective and recovery controls address security incidents and restore secure operations.

#### 4.1.1 Compromised Account Response

When a Salesforce identity is suspected of compromise, the organization should:

1. Validate the security alert.
2. Identify the affected identity.
3. Determine the account's privileges.
4. Restrict or disable access as appropriate.
5. Revoke affected sessions or tokens where supported.
6. Reset compromised credentials.
7. Review recent account activity.
8. Identify affected records and connected applications.
9. Preserve relevant evidence.
10. Restore access only after security validation.

For compromised integration identities, the response must include the connected middleware and dependent enterprise applications.

#### 4.1.2 Unauthorized Metadata Change Response

Unauthorized metadata modifications may affect application behavior and security configuration.

Corrective activities should include:

- Identify the affected metadata components.
- Determine who performed the changes.
- Compare the configuration with the approved baseline.
- Review the associated change records.
- Revert unauthorized modifications.
- Validate affected permissions and application behavior.
- Investigate related activities.
- Update preventive controls.

#### 4.1.3 Data Compromise Response

When customer information may have been exposed or modified, the organization should:

- Identify the affected data.
- Determine the scope of exposure.
- Identify affected users and applications.
- Establish whether information was accessed, modified or extracted.
- Contain ongoing unauthorized access.
- Preserve relevant evidence.
- Involve privacy and legal stakeholders where required.
- Evaluate notification obligations.
- Implement remediation.
- Update the risk register.

#### 4.1.4 Platform or Provider Incident Response

For incidents affecting Salesforce-managed infrastructure or shared platform services, the customer must coordinate with Salesforce through the applicable support and incident communication arrangements.

Customer response activities should include:

- Identify affected business services.
- Review provider communications.
- Evaluate business impact.
- Activate internal incident management procedures.
- Assess dependent integration failures.
- Communicate with affected business stakeholders.
- Implement approved workarounds.
- Validate service restoration.
- Document lessons learned.

The customer should not assume that it can directly remediate a provider-managed infrastructure incident.

#### 4.1.5 Data Recovery and Restoration

The organization should establish recovery capabilities appropriate to the criticality of its Salesforce data and business processes.

Potential capabilities include:

- Salesforce-supported data recovery services.
- Approved third-party backup solutions.
- Data export and restoration procedures.
- Metadata backup.
- Deployment and rollback procedures.
- Recovery of connected application data.
- Transaction reconciliation.

Backup and recovery requirements must be evaluated separately from Salesforce service availability.

The organization should define:

- Recovery Point Objective (RPO).
- Recovery Time Objective (RTO).
- Backup frequency.
- Data retention requirements.
- Restoration responsibilities.
- Recovery testing frequency.

### 1. Security Assessment Methodology

Security assessments of Salesforce platform architecture must determine whether the platform's security controls are appropriately designed, implemented and operating effectively.

The assessment should cover both Salesforce-managed platform dependencies and customer-managed security configurations.

A critical distinction must be maintained: an enterprise customer can assess its own Salesforce org, integrations and security processes, but cannot independently inspect or validate Salesforce's internal multi-tenant infrastructure without authorized provider evidence.

4. Application metadata and custom code are protected.
5. Data access is restricted according to business requirements.
6. APIs and integrations are securely configured.
7. Security events are monitored and investigated.
8. Platform and integration dependencies are addressed in resilience planning.
9. Security incidents can be detected, contained and recovered from.
10. Control effectiveness can be demonstrated through evidence.

4. **Collect evidence:** Obtain configurations, logs, provider assurance and operational records.
5. **Evaluate design:** Determine whether controls address identified risks.
6. **Test implementation:** Validate whether controls are correctly configured.
7. **Test operation:** Determine whether controls operate consistently.
8. **Identify gaps:** Document deviations, weaknesses and missing evidence.
9. **Assess risk:** Determine likelihood, impact and residual exposure.
10. **Report findings:** Assign severity, ownership and remediation requirements.
11. **Validate remediation:** Retest completed corrective actions.
12. **Maintain assurance:** Establish periodic reassessment.

### 2. Security Testing Procedures

Security testing validates whether Salesforce platform controls function as intended.

Testing should include configuration reviews, access testing, application security testing, API testing and operational control validation.

#### 2.1.1 Salesforce Organization Security Testing

The assessment team should examine:

- Authentication configuration.
- Multi-factor authentication enforcement.
- Session settings.
- Profiles and permission sets.
- Permission set groups.
- Organization-wide defaults.
- Role hierarchy.
- Sharing rules.
- Object permissions.
- Field-level security.
- Connected applications.
- Administrative privileges.
- Sandbox access.
- Security-relevant configuration changes.

Testing should use authorized test identities representing different business roles.

#### 2.1.2 Multi-Tenant Security Assurance Testing

Customers must distinguish between testing their own org's security boundaries and assessing Salesforce's underlying tenant isolation.

Customer-controlled testing should validate:

- Users cannot access unauthorized records.
- Users cannot access restricted objects.
- Sensitive fields are not exposed.
- Custom applications enforce authorization.
- API identities cannot exceed approved permissions.
- Sandbox environments do not expose production information unnecessarily.
- External applications cannot access unauthorized customer data.

Provider-managed tenant isolation should be evaluated through appropriate assurance reports, security documentation, contractual commitments and provider communications.

Customers must not conduct unauthorized testing against Salesforce's shared infrastructure or attempt to access other customers' environments.

#### 2.1.3 Application Security Testing

Custom Salesforce applications should undergo security testing before production deployment and periodically thereafter.

Testing should cover:

- Apex authorization.
- Sharing enforcement.
- Object and field permission checks.
- SOQL and SOSL injection.
- Input validation.
- Lightning Web Component security.
- Flow execution context.
- External callouts.
- Error handling.
- Sensitive information exposure.
- Application dependency security.

#### 2.1.4 API Security Testing

API testing should validate:

- Authentication requirements.
- OAuth authorization scopes.
- Integration identity permissions.
- Object-level access.
- Field-level access.
- Record-level access.
- Input validation.
- Rate and usage controls where applicable.
- Error handling.
- Credential protection.
- Token revocation.
- Unauthorized API request handling.

Testing must be conducted within approved environments and agreed testing boundaries.

#### 2.1.5 Configuration Baseline Testing

The organization should compare actual Salesforce security configurations against its approved baseline.

Examples include:

| Configuration Area | Testing Activity | Expected Result |
|---|---|---|
| Authentication | Validate required authentication controls | Approved authentication policy enforced |
| Administrative Access | Review privileged permissions | Only authorized administrators have elevated access |
| Object Permissions | Test unauthorized object access | Unauthorized operations denied |

| Connected Applications | Review application permissions | Approved scopes and permissions |

#### 3.1.1 Vulnerability Assessment Scope

Potential assessment targets include:

- Custom Apex applications.
- Lightning Web Components.
- Salesforce Experience Cloud configurations.
- Custom APIs and integrations.
- Middleware and integration services.
- Connected applications.
- External applications exchanging data with Salesforce.
- Enterprise identity integrations.

Penetration testing should focus on authorized customer-controlled assets.

### 4. Control Effectiveness Testing

Control effectiveness testing determines whether security controls achieve their intended objectives.

A control can be documented and configured but still fail to provide effective protection.

For this reason, testing should evaluate control design, implementation and operating effectiveness.

#### 4.1.1 Control Design Effectiveness

Design effectiveness evaluates whether a control is capable of addressing the identified risk.

Example:

A Salesforce organization requires protection against unauthorized access to sensitive customer records.

A control design that grants access to all employees and relies only on employee confidentiality agreements is insufficient.

The design should incorporate authorization restrictions, data classification and monitoring.

#### 4.1.2 Implementation Effectiveness

Implementation effectiveness determines whether the control has been correctly deployed.

Example:

The organization has approved a least-privilege access policy.

Testing should establish whether:

- The approved permission model exists.
- User permissions match approved roles.
- Sensitive objects are appropriately restricted.
- Sharing configurations reflect business requirements.
- Administrative privileges are limited.

#### 4.1.3 Operating Effectiveness

Operating effectiveness evaluates whether the control continues to function consistently over time.

Example:

A quarterly access review control should be tested by examining:

- Review schedules.
- Reviewer assignments.
- Completed access certifications.
- Identified access exceptions.
- Remediation records.
- Evidence of management oversight.

A policy document alone is not evidence that an access review operates effectively.

#### 4.1.4 Control Testing Matrix

| Control ID | Control Objective | Testing Procedure | Expected Result | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SF-CTL-001 | Enforce approved authentication | Review authentication configuration and test access | Required authentication enforced | Authentication configuration |
| SF-CTL-002 | Restrict privileged access | Review administrative accounts and approvals | Only authorized users have privileges | Privileged access review |
| SF-CTL-003 | Enforce least privilege | Compare permissions against approved access matrix | Permissions align with business roles | Access matrix and permission records |
| SF-CTL-004 | Protect sensitive records | Test record-level access | Unauthorized records inaccessible | Access test results |
| SF-CTL-005 | Protect sensitive fields | Test field-level permissions | Restricted fields inaccessible | Field security configuration |
| SF-CTL-006 | Protect metadata | Review deployment and change controls | Production changes authorized | Change and deployment records |
| SF-CTL-007 | Secure custom applications | Review code and security testing results | Security requirements implemented | Code review and test reports |
| SF-CTL-008 | Secure integrations | Review API identities and authorization | Integration access restricted | API configuration and access records |
| SF-CTL-009 | Monitor security events | Validate event ingestion and alert generation | Relevant events detected | SIEM test results |
| SF-CTL-010 | Manage provider dependency | Review provider assurance evidence | Provider risks assessed and governed | Third-party assessment |

#### 4.1.5 Control Deficiency Classification

Control deficiencies should be classified according to their risk and impact.

| Classification | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Critical | Severe weakness creating immediate exposure to highly sensitive data or critical business operations | Unrestricted privileged access to critical customer data |
| High | Significant control weakness with material security or business impact | Inadequate access restrictions for sensitive records |
| Medium | Control weakness that increases exposure but has compensating safeguards | Incomplete monitoring of selected administrative activities |
| Low | Limited control weakness with relatively low business impact | Incomplete documentation of a low-risk configuration review |

The organization should establish formal severity definitions and remediation timeframes.

- Salesforce event sources.
- Enterprise identity services.
- Middleware and API gateways.
- Custom applications.
- Cloud infrastructure.
- Endpoint security platforms.
- Data protection solutions.

The security operations function should correlate these events through the enterprise SIEM and other approved detection platforms.

### 6. Incident Response Procedures

Salesforce incidents may involve compromised accounts, unauthorized data access, malicious application changes, integration compromise or provider service disruption.

Incident response procedures should address both the Salesforce customer environment and external service dependencies.

#### 6.1.1 Salesforce Incident Classification

| Incident Type | Example | Primary Response Area |
|---|---|---|
| Identity Compromise | Unauthorized access to a Salesforce account | IAM and SOC |
| Privilege Abuse | Misuse of administrator permissions | SOC and Salesforce Administration |
| Data Exposure | Unauthorized access or export of customer information | Security, Privacy and Data Teams |
| Application Compromise | Exploitation of custom Apex or Lightning components | Application Security |
| Integration Compromise | Stolen credentials used by an integration identity | Integration and Security Teams |
| Configuration Compromise | Unauthorized changes to sharing or authentication settings | Salesforce Platform Team |
| Provider Incident | Salesforce-managed service or infrastructure incident | Provider Management and Business Continuity |
| Availability Incident | Salesforce or integration disruption affecting business processes | IT Operations and Business Continuity |

#### 6.1.2 Incident Response Lifecycle

The incident response process should include:

1. **Preparation:** Maintain incident procedures, contacts, access and response capabilities.
2. **Detection:** Identify suspicious events through monitoring and reporting.
3. **Analysis:** Determine incident scope, severity and affected assets.
4. **Containment:** Restrict compromised identities, applications or integrations.
5. **Eradication:** Remove malicious access and correct vulnerabilities.
6. **Recovery:** Restore secure service operation.
7. **Lessons Learned:** Identify root causes and improve controls.

#### 6.1.3 Example: Compromised Salesforce Administrator

**Scenario:**

The SOC identifies suspicious activity associated with a Salesforce administrator account.

The account has modified permission assignments and accessed sensitive customer records.

**Response procedures:**

| Phase | Response Activity | Responsible Function |
|---|---|---|
| Detection | Identify suspicious administrator activity | SOC |
| Analysis | Review authentication and administrative events | SOC and Salesforce Team |
| Containment | Restrict compromised account and revoke affected sessions where supported | IAM and Salesforce Team |
| Investigation | Identify permission changes and accessed records | SOC and Salesforce Team |
| Eradication | Remove unauthorized access and restore approved configuration | Salesforce Team |
| Recovery | Validate security configuration and restore approved access | Salesforce Platform Owner |
| Notification | Evaluate privacy, legal and regulatory obligations | Privacy and Legal Teams |
| Lessons Learned | Identify root cause and improve privileged access controls | Security Governance |

The organization should preserve evidence and maintain an auditable record of response decisions.

#### 6.1.4 Provider Incident Coordination

For incidents involving Salesforce-managed infrastructure, the customer should:

- Monitor official provider security communications.
- Open support cases through appropriate channels.
- Determine which business services are affected.
- Identify dependent integrations.
- Activate internal incident management.
- Evaluate customer and regulatory impact.
- Coordinate business continuity arrangements.
- Validate recovery.
- Document provider incident implications.

The customer must distinguish provider-confirmed facts from its own assumptions during incident investigation.

A multinational enterprise operates a Salesforce CRM environment supporting:

#### 11.1.2 Assessment Findings

| SF-FND-006 | Configuration changes are not consistently reconciled with approved change records | Unauthorized configuration changes | Strengthen configuration monitoring |
| SF-FND-007 | Provider assurance evidence is not periodically reviewed | Unmanaged provider dependency risk | Establish periodic provider assurance assessment |

#### 11.1.3 Remediation Plan

|---|---|

#### 11.1.5 Lessons Learned

The assessment demonstrates that Salesforce platform security cannot be established through a single configuration review or provider certification.

A reliable security posture requires:

- Clear provider and customer responsibility boundaries.
- Effective identity and authorization governance.
- Secure customer-managed application architecture.
- Protected integrations.
- Continuous monitoring.
- Controlled configuration changes.
- Tested incident response.
- Demonstrable control effectiveness.

Provider assurance is necessary, but it does not replace customer security governance.
