# 01.01 - Introduction to Salesforce CRM and Enterprise Cloud Computing

## Part 1 - Foundations, Concepts and Technology Architecture

### 1. Introduction to Salesforce CRM

Salesforce is a cloud-based Customer Relationship Management (CRM) platform that enables organizations to manage customer relationships, sales activities, service operations, marketing processes, customer information and digital engagement through a centralized technology ecosystem.

Unlike traditional CRM applications that organizations install and maintain entirely within their own data centers, Salesforce operates primarily through a Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) delivery model. Customers access the platform through the internet, while Salesforce operates and maintains the underlying cloud infrastructure and platform services.

Salesforce has evolved beyond traditional customer relationship management. It now provides a broad enterprise technology ecosystem that includes application development, data management, artificial intelligence, workflow automation, integration services, analytics and customer engagement capabilities.

Organizations use Salesforce to support business processes such as:

- Customer registration and account management.
- Sales opportunity and pipeline management.
- Customer service and support operations.
- Marketing campaign management.
- E-commerce and digital customer engagement.
- Customer data management and analytics.
- Workflow automation and business process orchestration.
- AI-powered customer interactions and business assistance.
- Integration with enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems.
- Customer identity, authentication and digital service delivery.

From a cybersecurity and governance perspective, Salesforce must be treated as a critical enterprise application platform rather than simply a customer database.

It can process sensitive customer information, commercially confidential data, personal information, transaction records and business-critical operational information. Its availability, integrity and confidentiality can directly affect business operations, regulatory compliance and customer trust.

Consequently, protecting Salesforce requires an understanding of its underlying technology, application architecture, data flows, integrations, identities, security configurations and operational dependencies.

### 2. Evolution of Salesforce and Enterprise CRM

Traditional CRM systems were originally designed to centralize customer information and support sales activities. Organizations commonly deployed these systems within their own infrastructure, requiring dedicated servers, databases, application software and internal IT resources.

These deployments created several operational responsibilities:

- Procuring and maintaining physical infrastructure.
- Installing and upgrading application software.
- Managing database infrastructure.
- Implementing network security.
- Maintaining application availability.
- Managing backups and disaster recovery.
- Applying security patches and configuration changes.
- Supporting application integrations.

The introduction of cloud computing changed this model.

Salesforce became one of the major providers of cloud-based CRM services, allowing organizations to consume CRM functionality through a subscription-based service rather than operating the entire application infrastructure themselves.

This transition introduced a different operating model:

| Traditional on-premises CRM                                                         | Cloud-based Salesforce CRM                                                                   |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Organization operates application infrastructure.                                   | Salesforce operates the underlying SaaS infrastructure.                                      |
| Internal IT manages servers and databases.                                          | Salesforce manages the underlying platform infrastructure.                                   |
| Organization controls application installation and upgrades.                        | Salesforce manages platform releases and service updates.                                    |
| Organization manages infrastructure availability.                                   | Salesforce provides the underlying service availability commitments.                         |
| Internal IT manages infrastructure-level maintenance.                               | Responsibilities are divided between Salesforce and the customer.                            |
| Security is implemented across internally operated infrastructure and applications. | Security responsibilities are shared across Salesforce, the customer and external providers. |

This transition does not eliminate the organization's cybersecurity responsibilities.

Instead, it changes where those responsibilities exist and how they must be governed.

For example, Salesforce may operate the underlying cloud infrastructure, but the customer remains responsible for appropriately configuring user permissions, controlling access to customer records, managing integrations, protecting credentials and establishing suitable security governance.

This distinction is fundamental to Salesforce cybersecurity risk management.

### 3. Understanding Salesforce as a Software-as-a-Service Platform

Software as a Service (SaaS) is a cloud computing delivery model in which a provider makes an application available to customers over a network, generally through a subscription-based commercial arrangement.

The provider operates the underlying service infrastructure and maintains the application environment.

Customers consume the service without having to independently install and maintain the complete application stack.

Salesforce CRM is primarily delivered through this model.

A typical Salesforce SaaS environment involves three major responsibility areas:

1\. Salesforce service provider

Salesforce operates and maintains the underlying service infrastructure and platform according to the applicable product architecture and contractual commitments.

2\. Customer organization

The customer configures and administers its Salesforce environment, manages users and permissions, governs its business data, controls integrations and implements appropriate organizational security requirements.

3\. Third-party technology providers

External providers may supply connected applications, middleware, identity services, AI models, payment services, analytics platforms and other technologies that interact with Salesforce.

These parties introduce additional security dependencies that must be evaluated.

#### 3.1 SaaS Service Characteristics

The Salesforce SaaS model generally provides the following characteristics:

| Characteristic                   | Description                                                                                                                                          |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Subscription-based consumption   | Organizations subscribe to Salesforce services and applicable products.                                                                              |
| Internet-based access            | Authorized users access the platform through supported network connections.                                                                          |
| Provider-managed infrastructure  | Salesforce operates the underlying infrastructure supporting its services.                                                                           |
| Shared platform services         | Customers consume services running on Salesforce-managed infrastructure.                                                                             |
| Centralized platform maintenance | Salesforce manages applicable platform maintenance and releases.                                                                                     |
| Configurable applications        | Customers configure applications and business processes according to their requirements.                                                             |
| Integration capabilities         | Salesforce supports connections with external applications and enterprise systems.                                                                   |
| Service scalability              | Organizations can use available platform capabilities to support changing business requirements, subject to product limits and service architecture. |

#### 3.2 SaaS Security Implications

The SaaS model introduces specific cybersecurity considerations.

First, the customer does not have unrestricted administrative access to the underlying physical infrastructure or operating systems supporting Salesforce.

Second, the organization must understand which security controls are operated by Salesforce and which must be configured and maintained by the customer.

Third, the organization depends on the provider's service availability, security architecture, operational controls and contractual commitments.

Fourth, integrations with external systems can extend the Salesforce attack surface beyond the Salesforce platform itself.

For example, an organization may operate Salesforce CRM with connections to an e-commerce platform, SAP S/4HANA ERP and an external payment service.

Although Salesforce is delivered as SaaS, the overall enterprise solution consists of multiple interconnected technology environments.

A security weakness in an external integration could therefore affect the confidentiality, integrity or availability of information processed by Salesforce.

### 4. Cloud Computing Fundamentals and Service Delivery Models

Cloud computing is the delivery of computing resources and services through network-accessible infrastructure rather than requiring organizations to operate all computing resources locally.

Cloud services can include:

- Computing and processing resources.
- Storage and database services.
- Networking and connectivity.
- Application hosting.
- Development platforms.
- Security services.
- Analytics and artificial intelligence.
- Business applications.

Cloud computing is commonly divided into three primary service delivery models: Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS), Platform as a Service (PaaS) and Software as a Service (SaaS).

#### 4.1 Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS)

IaaS provides virtualized infrastructure resources through a cloud provider.

The customer typically manages operating systems, applications, middleware and relevant configurations, while the provider manages the underlying physical infrastructure and virtualization services.

Examples include:

- Amazon Web Services (AWS) EC2.
- Microsoft Azure Virtual Machines.
- Google Cloud Compute Engine.

Security responsibilities:

Organizations must manage operating system hardening, application security, identity permissions, network configurations, vulnerability remediation and data protection according to the service model.

#### 4.2 Platform as a Service (PaaS)

PaaS provides an environment for developing, deploying and operating applications without requiring customers to manage the complete underlying infrastructure.

The provider manages the platform infrastructure and applicable runtime services, while customers remain responsible for their applications, code, data and configurations according to the platform's responsibility model.

Examples include:

- Salesforce Platform.
- Microsoft Azure App Service.
- Google App Engine.

Salesforce provides platform capabilities that allow organizations to create custom applications, configure business processes, develop Apex code and build Lightning components.

#### 4.3 Software as a Service (SaaS)

SaaS provides complete application services that customers access through supported interfaces.

Examples include:

- Salesforce CRM.
- Microsoft 365.
- ServiceNow.
- Google Workspace.

In the SaaS model, the provider operates the underlying application service and infrastructure. Customers configure the application and manage their own data, identities, permissions and business processes.

#### 4.4 Comparison of Cloud Service Models

| Responsibility            | IaaS     | PaaS     | SaaS                                                         |
| ------------------------- | -------- | -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Physical infrastructure   | Provider | Provider | Provider                                                     |
| Virtualization            | Provider | Provider | Provider                                                     |
| Operating system          | Customer | Provider | Provider                                                     |
| Runtime and middleware    | Customer | Provider | Provider                                                     |
| Application               | Customer | Customer | Provider                                                     |
| Application configuration | Customer | Customer | Shared, depending on service                                 |
| Data governance           | Customer | Customer | Customer responsibility with provider-supported capabilities |
| User access management    | Customer | Customer | Shared, with customer-managed access configuration           |

The exact allocation of responsibilities varies by provider and service.

#### 4.5 Why This Matters for Salesforce Security

Understanding the cloud service model is essential when conducting Salesforce cybersecurity risk assessments.

An organization should not assume that because Salesforce manages the infrastructure, all security responsibilities are automatically covered.

For example:

- Salesforce manages the underlying platform infrastructure.
- The customer configures Salesforce users and permissions.
- The customer governs the information stored in its Salesforce organization.
- The customer secures custom-developed applications and integrations.
- The customer evaluates external applications connected to Salesforce.
- Both parties have responsibilities during security incidents, according to their respective contractual and operational obligations.

Salesforce's Well-Architected Framework identifies Trust, Reliability, Operational Excellence, Resource and Cost Optimization, and Fairness as its five architectural pillars. These provide a useful foundation for evaluating the quality and security of Salesforce solutions.&#x20;

### 5. Salesforce Platform Overview and Core Technology Components

Salesforce is a configurable cloud platform that combines application functionality, data management, automation, development capabilities and integration services.

An enterprise Salesforce implementation can contain multiple interconnected components.

#### 5.1 Salesforce Organization (Org)

A Salesforce organization, commonly called an org, is the logical environment in which an organization operates its Salesforce applications, data, users, configurations and customizations.

An org contains components such as:

- User accounts.
- Profiles and permission sets.
- Business objects and records.
- Applications and application configurations.
- Custom fields and objects.
- Automation workflows.
- Apex code.
- Lightning components.
- Security configurations.
- Integration configurations.

From a cybersecurity perspective, the org is an important unit of security administration and assessment.

An organization must establish appropriate controls over its org configuration, users, data, applications and external connections.

#### 5.2 Salesforce Database and Data Model

Salesforce uses a platform-managed data architecture that organizes business information into objects, fields and records.

The primary concepts are:

| Component     | Description                                                          | Example                     |
| ------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Object        | Structure that stores a particular category of business information. | Account                     |
| Field         | Attribute associated with an object.                                 | Account Name                |
| Record        | Individual data entry within an object.                              | A specific customer account |
| Relationship  | Association between objects.                                         | Contact linked to Account   |
| Custom Object | Customer-created business data structure.                            | Customer_Contract__c        |
| Custom Field  | Customer-created data attribute.                                     | Customer_ID__c              |

Salesforce provides standard objects, such as Account, Contact, Opportunity, Case and Lead, alongside custom objects created to support organization-specific requirements.

Security relevance:

A poorly designed data model can introduce security and governance weaknesses.

Examples include:

- Storing sensitive information in fields without appropriate access restrictions.
- Creating custom objects without considering data classification.
- Allowing excessive record access through sharing configurations.
- Storing authentication credentials in inappropriate fields.
- Exposing sensitive customer information through reports or dashboards.
- Retaining personal information longer than necessary.

Data architecture must therefore be considered during cybersecurity assessments, not treated solely as an application development concern.

#### 5.3 Salesforce Application Layer

The application layer contains the business functionality through which users interact with Salesforce.

Examples include:

- Sales processes.
- Customer service workflows.
- Marketing activities.
- Customer account management.
- Case management.
- Business automation.
- Custom business applications.

Applications can use standard Salesforce functionality or custom-developed components.

The security assessment must consider how these applications process information, enforce permissions and interact with other platform components.

#### 5.4 Salesforce Automation and Business Logic

Salesforce supports automation capabilities that allow organizations to execute business processes without requiring every action to be performed manually.

Examples include:

- Salesforce Flow.
- Approval Processes.
- Validation Rules.
- Apex Triggers.
- Scheduled automation.
- Event-driven processing.

These capabilities introduce additional security considerations.

For example, an automated process may access customer records, modify account information, initiate business actions or communicate with external systems.

Security reviews should evaluate:

- Which user or execution context runs the automation.
- What records and fields it can access.
- Whether the automation enforces appropriate permissions.
- Whether input validation is implemented.
- Whether its actions are logged.
- Whether changes are authorized and tested.

#### 5.5 Salesforce Development and Customization

Salesforce provides development capabilities that enable organizations to customize the platform.

Important technologies include:

| Technology               | Purpose                                                          |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Apex                     | Salesforce server-side programming language.                     |
| Lightning Web Components | Framework for developing user interface components.              |
| Visualforce              | Technology for developing customized Salesforce pages.           |
| Salesforce APIs          | Interfaces for connecting Salesforce with external applications. |
| Salesforce Metadata      | Definitions and configurations of platform components.           |
| Salesforce Flow          | Declarative automation and business process execution.           |

Custom development increases the organization's responsibility for application security.

For example, insecure Apex code may introduce SOQL injection, authorization weaknesses or inappropriate access to sensitive records.

Custom Lightning components may also introduce client-side security weaknesses if data is improperly handled or exposed.

These risks must be addressed through secure development practices, code reviews and appropriate security testing.

### 7. Enterprise CRM Architecture and Business Process Integration

Salesforce is frequently implemented as part of a larger enterprise application environment.

It may act as the central customer relationship platform while relying on other applications to execute business processes.

For example, a telecommunications company may use Salesforce to manage customer interactions while relying on SAP S/4HANA for enterprise resource planning, an e-commerce platform for online purchases and separate operational systems for service provisioning.

This means that Salesforce security cannot be evaluated independently of the surrounding enterprise architecture.

#### 7.1 Typical Enterprise Application Architecture

A conceptual enterprise CRM environment may contain the following components:

| Layer                | Technology or application                | Business function                           |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Customer interaction | Website, mobile app, customer portal     | Customer engagement                         |
| CRM application      | Salesforce Sales Cloud and Service Cloud | Customer relationship management            |
| Customer data        | Salesforce Data Cloud                    | Customer data unification                   |
| Integration          | MuleSoft and API services                | Enterprise connectivity                     |
| ERP                  | SAP S/4HANA                              | Finance, inventory and enterprise resources |
| E-commerce           | Digital commerce platform                | Online transactions                         |
| Identity             | Enterprise identity provider             | Authentication and identity federation      |
| AI                   | Agentforce and external LLMs             | AI-powered interactions                     |
| Security operations  | SIEM, XDR and SOAR                       | Monitoring and incident response            |

#### 7.2 Example: Customer Registration Process

Consider a customer registering through an organization's website.

A simplified process may involve:

1. The customer accesses the website.
2. The customer submits registration information.
3. The website sends the information to an API integration layer.
4. The integration layer validates and processes the request.
5. Salesforce creates or updates the customer record.
6. The CRM application associates the customer with relevant business processes.
7. Customer information may be synchronized with other enterprise applications.
8. Security monitoring systems collect available events from the connected environments.

Each step introduces potential security considerations.

For example, if the API does not properly authenticate requests or validate submitted information, an attacker may attempt to manipulate customer records or submit unauthorized transactions.

#### 7.3 Example: Customer Order Processing

A second example involves an online customer purchase.

1. The customer accesses the e-commerce platform.
2. The customer selects a product or service.
3. The e-commerce application submits the order information.
4. The integration layer communicates with Salesforce.
5. Salesforce updates the customer and order-related business records.
6. SAP S/4HANA processes relevant ERP activities.
7. The order status is synchronized with connected applications.
8. Salesforce may provide customer service visibility into the transaction.

Security assessments should examine the integrity of order information, authentication of integration services, API permissions, data confidentiality and transaction monitoring.

#### 7.4 Security Implications of Enterprise Integration

Enterprise integration increases the number of trust relationships and potential attack paths.

Consider the following scenarios:

| Integration                     | Potential security risk                                               |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Salesforce to SAP S/4HANA       | Unauthorized modification of business or customer-related information |
| Salesforce to e-commerce        | Manipulation of customer or order data                                |
| Salesforce to payment gateway   | Exposure or misuse of payment-related information                     |
| Salesforce to customer portal   | Unauthorized access to customer records                               |
| Salesforce to identity provider | Account compromise or authentication failure                          |
| Salesforce to external LLM      | Sensitive data disclosure                                             |
| Salesforce to SIEM              | Incomplete security event visibility                                  |
| Salesforce to third-party SaaS  | Vendor compromise or unauthorized data processing                     |

A complete Salesforce cybersecurity assessment must therefore consider both the internal Salesforce environment and its external technology dependencies.

### 8. Salesforce Data Architecture and Information Lifecycle

Data is one of the most important assets within a Salesforce environment.

Organizations use Salesforce to store, process and exchange information that supports customer relationships and business operations.

A security assessment must consider the entire information lifecycle.

#### 8.1 Data Lifecycle

A typical data lifecycle consists of:

1. Data collection.
2. Data ingestion.
3. Data validation.
4. Data processing.
5. Data storage.
6. Data access and usage.
7. Data sharing and transmission.
8. Data retention.
9. Data archival.
10. Data deletion or disposal.

Each stage introduces different security and privacy requirements.

For example, collecting unnecessary personal information increases privacy exposure, while retaining sensitive information beyond its required retention period increases the consequences of a potential data breach.

#### 8.2 Data Classification

Organizations should establish a data classification model to determine the appropriate protection requirements for information processed through Salesforce.

An illustrative classification model is:

| Classification | Description                                   | Example                                        | Protection requirements                                                                  |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Public         | Information approved for public disclosure    | Public product information                     | Appropriate integrity and availability controls                                          |
| Internal       | Information intended for authorized employees | Internal business notes                        | Authenticated access and appropriate permissions                                         |
| Confidential   | Sensitive commercial or customer information  | Customer financial information                 | Restricted access, encryption where appropriate and monitoring                           |
| Restricted     | Highly sensitive or regulated information     | Health information and sensitive identity data | Strong access restrictions, appropriate encryption, auditability and enhanced monitoring |

This classification model is an enterprise reference example, not a mandatory Salesforce classification scheme.

#### 8.3 Data Security Considerations

Important considerations include:

- Data ownership and accountability.
- Data classification.
- Data minimization.
- Access authorization.
- Encryption in transit and at rest.
- Data retention and deletion.
- Data residency requirements.
- Data sharing with external providers.
- Data loss prevention.
- Privacy and consent management.
- Data backup and recovery.
- Data integrity and auditability.

Salesforce provides platform capabilities that support several of these requirements, but the customer must determine how to configure and govern them.

#### 8.4 Data Flows and Trust Boundaries

A trust boundary represents a point where information crosses between different security domains, systems, applications or privilege levels.

Examples include:

- Customer browser to Salesforce Experience Cloud.
- Salesforce to MuleSoft.
- MuleSoft to SAP S/4HANA.
- Salesforce to an external LLM.
- Salesforce to a payment provider.
- Salesforce to an enterprise SIEM.
- Salesforce to a third-party application.

Every trust boundary should be assessed for authentication, authorization, encryption, data validation, logging and monitoring.

This is especially important when an integration transfers customer information or allows one system to initiate actions in another.

### 9. Enterprise Cloud Computing Security and Shared Responsibility

#### 9.4 Why Shared Responsibility Matters for Risk Assessment

A common mistake in cloud security is assuming that the cloud provider is responsible for every security control.

This creates a potential governance gap.

For example, Salesforce may provide the platform infrastructure and security capabilities, but an organization might configure overly permissive access to customer records.

The infrastructure may be operating securely while the customer's Salesforce implementation remains exposed.

A cybersecurity risk assessment must therefore identify:

1. Which security controls are provided by Salesforce.
2. Which controls require customer configuration.
3. Which controls depend on external technology providers.
4. Which controls require collaboration.
5. Who owns each control.
6. What evidence demonstrates that each control operates effectively.

The shared responsibility model must be documented and communicated to the relevant business, technology, security and compliance stakeholders.

### 2. Threat Landscape

The Salesforce threat landscape includes external attackers, malicious insiders, compromised identities, vulnerable integrations, insecure applications and operational failures.

Because Salesforce is accessible through cloud-based interfaces and APIs, attackers may target both the platform's user-facing components and the organization's connected enterprise environment.

#### 2.1.1 External Threat Actors

External threat actors may attempt to compromise Salesforce environments for financial gain, data theft, fraud, espionage or disruption.

Common threat actor categories include:

**Cybercriminal Groups**

Cybercriminals may target Salesforce environments to obtain customer information, steal commercially valuable data, conduct fraud or exploit compromised accounts.

Potential techniques include:

- Credential theft.
- Phishing.
- Password spraying.
- Session hijacking.
- Exploitation of vulnerable custom applications.
- Abuse of exposed APIs.
- Unauthorized data extraction.

**Advanced Persistent Threat Groups**

Sophisticated threat actors may target organizations handling valuable customer, commercial or strategic information.

Potential techniques include:

- Targeted phishing.
- Compromise of privileged identities.
- Abuse of trusted integrations.
- Persistence through compromised application credentials.
- Data exfiltration through legitimate application interfaces.

**Opportunistic Attackers**

Opportunistic attackers may exploit publicly accessible endpoints, weak authentication configurations, exposed credentials or vulnerable custom applications.

Their activities may include automated scanning, credential attacks and exploitation of known software vulnerabilities.

#### 2.1.2 Internal Threat Actors

Internal threats originate from individuals who already have legitimate access to the organization's systems.

These threats may be intentional or accidental.

Examples include:

- Employees accessing customer records beyond their business responsibilities.
- Administrators granting excessive permissions.
- Developers introducing insecure application code.
- Contractors retaining access after completing their assignments.
- Employees exporting customer information without authorization.
- Users unintentionally disclosing sensitive information through external applications.

An important distinction is that legitimate access does not automatically mean authorized business use.

A Salesforce user may have technically valid access to a record while using that access for an unauthorized purpose.

#### 2.1.3 Compromised Identities

Identity compromise is a significant risk for SaaS environments because authentication determines access to business applications and data.

An attacker who obtains a valid Salesforce user's credentials may be able to operate through legitimate application interfaces.

Potential attack techniques include:

- Phishing and credential harvesting.
- Password reuse.
- Credential stuffing.
- Password spraying.
- Theft of authentication tokens.
- Session hijacking.
- Abuse of compromised OAuth credentials.
- Compromise of administrator accounts.

The severity of an identity compromise depends on the account's privileges, accessible data, connected applications and available security controls.

A compromised standard user account may expose a limited set of records, whereas a compromised administrator account may create opportunities for broader configuration changes and access expansion.

### 5. Attack Vectors and Exploitation Scenarios

An attack vector is the path or method used by a threat actor to exploit a vulnerability or compromise an asset.

The following scenarios illustrate how Salesforce-related attacks may occur in an enterprise environment.

#### 5.1.1 Credential Phishing Attack

**Scenario:**

An employee receives a fraudulent email designed to resemble a legitimate corporate authentication notification.

The employee follows the malicious link and submits their Salesforce credentials to a phishing website.

The attacker obtains the credentials and attempts to access the Salesforce environment.

**Potential attack sequence:**

1. The attacker identifies an employee with Salesforce access.
2. A phishing message is delivered.
3. The employee submits authentication credentials.
4. The attacker attempts to authenticate using the stolen credentials.
5. If authentication protections are insufficient, the attacker gains access.
6. The attacker accesses available customer records or business information.
7. The attacker attempts to expand access or extract information.

**Potential impact:**

- Unauthorized access to customer data.
- Exposure of confidential business information.
- Fraudulent modification of CRM records.
- Compromise of connected business processes.

**Security considerations:**

- Strong authentication.
- Phishing-resistant authentication where feasible.
- Conditional access and identity risk controls.
- Session security.
- User awareness.
- Authentication and activity monitoring.

#### 5.1.2 Excessive Privilege Exploitation

**Scenario:**

A user receives permissions beyond their business responsibilities.

The user can access sensitive customer records, modify business information or perform administrative activities that are not necessary for their role.

An attacker who compromises this account inherits the excessive privileges.

**Potential attack sequence:**

1. An account is assigned excessive permissions.
2. The account is compromised through phishing or credential theft.
3. The attacker authenticates using the legitimate account.
4. The attacker accesses information outside the user's actual business responsibilities.
5. The attacker exports or modifies accessible records.

**Potential impact:**

- Unauthorized disclosure of customer information.
- Unauthorized business transactions.
- Increased blast radius of an account compromise.
- Potential regulatory and contractual consequences.

**Security considerations:**

- Least privilege.
- Role-based access design.
- Permission-set governance.
- Periodic access reviews.
- Privileged access management.
- Monitoring of sensitive operations.

#### 5.1.3 Insecure API Integration Exploitation

**Scenario:**

A Salesforce integration uses an account with excessive privileges and credentials that are inadequately protected.

An attacker obtains the integration credentials through a compromised middleware environment.

**Potential attack sequence:**

1. The attacker compromises the middleware environment.
2. Integration credentials are obtained.
3. The attacker authenticates to Salesforce through the integration interface.
4. The attacker performs operations permitted by the integration identity.
5. Customer or business data is accessed, modified or extracted.

**Potential impact:**

- Unauthorized data access.
- Customer record manipulation.
- Disruption of enterprise processes.
- Exposure of information across connected systems.

**Security considerations:**

- Dedicated integration identities.
- Least-privilege API permissions.
- Secure credential storage.
- OAuth governance.
- Credential rotation where appropriate.
- API activity monitoring.
- Network and application-layer protections.

#### 5.1.4 Data Export and Exfiltration

**Scenario:**

An employee with legitimate Salesforce access exports a large volume of customer information without an approved business purpose.

The exported information is transferred to an unauthorized external destination.

**Potential attack sequence:**

1. The user authenticates to Salesforce.
2. The user accesses customer records.
3. The user performs a bulk export or uses an available data extraction mechanism.
4. The information is downloaded.
5. The information is transferred to an unauthorized destination.

**Potential impact:**

- Customer privacy violations.
- Loss of commercially sensitive information.
- Regulatory exposure.
- Reputational damage.
- Contractual and legal consequences.

**Security considerations:**

- Data classification.
- Restriction of unnecessary export privileges.
- Monitoring of bulk data access.
- Data loss prevention controls.
- User activity monitoring.
- Investigation of anomalous data access.

#### 5.1.5 Compromise of a Custom Salesforce Application

**Scenario:**

A custom Apex application contains an authorization weakness that allows a user to access records beyond their intended permissions.

**Potential attack sequence:**

1. A developer implements custom application functionality.
2. Authorization checks are incomplete or incorrectly implemented.
3. A user interacts with the application.
4. The application performs an operation without adequately enforcing the intended access restrictions.
5. Sensitive records become accessible to unauthorized users.

**Potential impact:**

- Unauthorized record access.
- Exposure of sensitive information.
- Business process manipulation.
- Increased application compromise risk.

**Security considerations:**

- Secure Apex development.
- Explicit authorization checks.
- Appropriate sharing behavior.
- Object and field permission enforcement.
- Code review.
- Static analysis.
- Security testing.

#### 5.1.6 Third-Party Application Compromise

**Scenario:**

An organization installs a third-party Salesforce application to extend CRM functionality.

The application requests extensive permissions and introduces an additional trusted component into the environment.

The third-party application or its service provider is subsequently compromised.

**Potential attack sequence:**

1. A third-party application is selected and installed.
2. Permissions are granted to the application.
3. The application receives access to Salesforce data or functionality.
4. The provider experiences a security compromise.
5. The attacker abuses the application's authorized access or associated integration mechanisms.

**Potential impact:**

- Unauthorized data disclosure.
- Compromise of connected business processes.
- Increased attack surface.
- Supply-chain security exposure.

**Security considerations:**

- Third-party security assessment.
- AppExchange application review.
- OAuth scope assessment.
- Contractual security requirements.
- Ongoing vendor monitoring.
- Periodic review of connected applications.

### 7. Business Impact Analysis

Cybersecurity risks affecting Salesforce must be evaluated according to their potential consequences for business operations.

A technical vulnerability does not automatically represent a critical business risk. Its severity depends on the affected asset, exposure, exploitability, business dependency and potential consequences.

#### 7.1.1 Confidentiality Impact

Confidentiality impact occurs when information is disclosed to unauthorized individuals, systems or organizations.

Potential consequences include:

- Exposure of customer personal information.
- Disclosure of commercially sensitive information.
- Unauthorized access to sales pipelines.
- Exposure of customer service records.
- Leakage of authentication or integration credentials.
- Disclosure of confidential business documents.

The organization must consider the sensitivity, volume and regulatory classification of the affected information.

#### 7.1.2 Integrity Impact

Integrity impact occurs when information or system configurations are modified without appropriate authorization.

Potential consequences include:

- Manipulation of customer records.
- Modification of sales opportunities.
- Unauthorized changes to customer account information.
- Incorrect order-related information.
- Manipulation of integration data.
- Unauthorized changes to application configurations.

Integrity failures may create financial, operational and reputational consequences even when no information is disclosed.

#### 7.1.3 Availability Impact

Availability impact occurs when Salesforce or a dependent service becomes unavailable or cannot perform its intended business functions.

Potential causes include:

- Salesforce service disruption.
- Identity provider outages.
- Integration failures.
- API limits or capacity constraints.
- Malicious activity.
- Incorrect production configuration changes.
- Failures in connected enterprise applications.

Potential consequences include:

- Customer service interruption.
- Delayed sales operations.
- Inability to access customer information.
- Delayed order processing.
- Disruption of digital customer experiences.
- Increased operational costs.

Availability assessments must distinguish between Salesforce service availability and the availability of the organization's complete business process.

A Salesforce service may remain operational while a critical integration failure prevents the business from completing an end-to-end transaction.

#### 7.1.4 Regulatory and Legal Impact

Salesforce may process personal information and other regulated business data.

Depending on the organization's activities and the data involved, security incidents may create obligations under applicable privacy, cybersecurity and sector-specific requirements.

Potential consequences include:

- Regulatory investigations.
- Mandatory breach notifications.
- Contractual disputes.
- Legal claims.
- Financial penalties where applicable.
- Increased audit scrutiny.

Regulatory exposure must be assessed against the organization's actual legal obligations and processing activities rather than assumed solely because Salesforce is being used.

#### 7.1.5 Financial Impact

Financial consequences may include:

- Incident response expenses.
- Business interruption costs.
- Recovery and remediation expenses.
- Legal and regulatory costs.
- Customer compensation.
- Loss of revenue.
- Increased insurance premiums.
- Additional investment in security controls.

The financial impact may extend beyond the Salesforce platform itself because connected enterprise applications and business processes can also be affected.

#### 7.1.6 Reputational Impact

A Salesforce-related security incident may affect customer confidence and the organization's reputation.

Examples include:

- Public disclosure of a customer data breach.
- Unauthorized access to customer accounts.
- Exposure of sensitive commercial information.
- Disruption of customer support services.
- Inability to process customer requests.
- Repeated security incidents caused by inadequate governance.

### 8. Risk Assessment Methodology

The assessment should consider the organization's business context, Salesforce architecture, data sensitivity, security controls, integration dependencies and operational requirements.

#### 8.1.1 Establish the Assessment Scope

The first step is to define the boundaries of the Salesforce security assessment.

The scope may include:

- Salesforce production environments.
- Salesforce sandbox environments.
- Sales Cloud.
- Service Cloud.
- Experience Cloud.
- Marketing Cloud.
- Data Cloud.
- Salesforce Shield capabilities.
- Custom Salesforce applications.
- APIs and connected applications.
- MuleSoft and other integration services.
- Third-party applications.
- Identity and access management.
- Security monitoring and incident response.

The assessment must also identify the business processes supported by the environment.

#### 8.1.2 Identify Critical Assets

Threat identification determines which threat events could affect the identified assets.

Examples include:

- Third-party compromise.
- Data exfiltration.
- Ransomware affecting connected enterprise systems.
- AI prompt manipulation.
- Service disruption.

The assessment identifies weaknesses that could enable the identified threats.

Sources of vulnerability information may include:

- API security assessments.
- Penetration testing.
- Vulnerability assessments.
- Third-party security reviews.

#### 8.1.5 Evaluate Existing Controls

Existing controls must be identified before determining the level of risk remaining in the environment.

Examples include:

### 9. Inherent and Residual Risk

Inherent risk and residual risk represent two different stages of risk evaluation.

**Inherent Risk** is the level of risk before considering the effect of existing controls.

**Residual Risk** is the level of risk remaining after considering the design and operating effectiveness of existing controls.

#### 9.1.1 Inherent Risk Assessment

Inherent risk considers the threat scenario without relying on existing safeguards to reduce its likelihood or impact.

For example:

A Salesforce production environment contains a large volume of customer records and is connected to multiple enterprise applications.

If an attacker compromises a privileged account, the potential impact could be substantial.

The inherent risk may therefore be high or critical, depending on the organization's assessment criteria.

#### 9.1.2 Existing Control Assessment

The organization evaluates whether existing controls reduce the likelihood or impact of the risk.

Example controls:

- Phishing-resistant authentication.
- Privileged identity restrictions.
- Least-privilege permission design.
- Monitoring of administrative activities.
- Alerts for suspicious access patterns.
- Data export restrictions.
- Incident response procedures.

The effectiveness of each control must be supported by evidence.

#### 9.1.3 Residual Risk Assessment

Residual risk reflects the remaining exposure after existing controls have been evaluated.

For example:

| Assessment Element | Example |
|---|---|
| Risk Scenario | Compromise of a Salesforce administrator account |
| Inherent Likelihood | High |
| Inherent Impact | Critical |
| Inherent Risk | Critical |
| Existing Controls | Strong authentication, privileged access restrictions and monitoring |
| Control Effectiveness | Partially effective |
| Remaining Control Gap | Incomplete monitoring of privileged activity |
| Residual Likelihood | Medium |
| Residual Impact | Critical |
| Residual Risk | High |

The example demonstrates that effective preventive controls may reduce the likelihood of an incident without necessarily reducing the potential impact if the incident occurs.

#### 9.1.4 Risk Acceptance

Residual risks must be evaluated against the organization's risk appetite and tolerance.

Where residual risk exceeds the approved threshold, management should consider:

- Additional risk mitigation.
- Risk avoidance.
- Risk transfer where appropriate.
- Formal risk acceptance by an authorized risk owner.

Risk acceptance must be documented and supported by an appropriate business rationale.

### 10. Risk Scoring and Prioritization

Risk scoring provides a consistent mechanism for comparing and prioritizing Salesforce cybersecurity risks.

A commonly used approach evaluates likelihood and impact.

#### 10.1.1 Likelihood Assessment

Likelihood measures the probability that a risk scenario will occur within a defined assessment period.

| Rating | Level | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Rare | Highly unlikely under current conditions |
| 2 | Unlikely | Possible but not expected to occur frequently |
| 3 | Possible | Could occur under realistic circumstances |
| 4 | Likely | Expected to occur in many circumstances |
| 5 | Almost Certain | Expected to occur frequently or repeatedly |

Factors that may influence likelihood include:

- Threat actor capability.
- Exposure of the affected service.
- Vulnerability severity.
- Availability of exploit techniques.
- Existing security controls.
- Frequency of access.
- Historical incidents.
- Integration complexity.
- External threat intelligence.

#### 10.1.2 Impact Assessment

Impact measures the potential business consequences if the risk scenario occurs.

| Rating | Level | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Insignificant | Minimal operational or business consequences |
| 2 | Minor | Limited disruption or information exposure |
| 3 | Moderate | Noticeable operational or financial impact |
| 4 | Major | Significant business disruption or data exposure |
| 5 | Critical | Severe business, regulatory, financial or reputational consequences |

Impact should consider:

- Confidentiality.
- Integrity.
- Availability.
- Financial loss.
- Regulatory exposure.
- Customer impact.
- Operational disruption.
- Reputational damage.

#### 10.1.3 Risk Calculation

A simple qualitative risk model can use the following formula:

**Risk Score = Likelihood × Impact**

The resulting score ranges from 1 to 25.

| Risk Score | Risk Level | Suggested Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | Low | Maintain existing controls and monitor |
| 5–9 | Medium | Evaluate control improvements and monitor |
| 10–16 | High | Prioritize remediation and management oversight |
| 17–25 | Critical | Immediate management attention and urgent risk treatment |

The organization should formally approve its scoring methodology and thresholds.

This model is a proposed assessment approach, not a mandatory Salesforce scoring standard.

#### 10.1.4 Example Risk Calculation

**Scenario:** Excessive access to Salesforce customer records.

| Assessment Element | Rating |
|---|---|
| Likelihood | 4 - Likely |
| Impact | 5 - Critical |
| Risk Score | 20 |
| Risk Level | Critical |

Calculation:

20 = 4 × 5

The organization should prioritize this risk because unauthorized access could expose a significant volume of customer information.

Potential mitigation measures include:

- Reviewing organization-wide defaults.
- Restricting object and field permissions.
- Reviewing sharing rules.
- Implementing least-privilege access.
- Conducting periodic access certification.
- Monitoring sensitive data access.

After implementation, the organization should reassess likelihood and impact to determine residual risk.

## Part 3 - Security Controls, Mitigation and Implementation

### 1. Security Control Framework

Salesforce cybersecurity controls must protect the platform, customer information, identities, applications, integrations and business processes against identified threats.

A comprehensive control framework should combine Salesforce-native security capabilities, enterprise security technologies, organizational policies and operational procedures.

The control framework should be designed around three primary control categories:

- **Preventive controls:** Reduce the likelihood of security incidents by preventing unauthorized activities.
- **Detective controls:** Identify suspicious activities, security violations and potential incidents.
- **Corrective and recovery controls:** Contain incidents, restore services and reduce the consequences of security failures.

These controls should be implemented according to the organization's risk profile, Salesforce architecture, business requirements and regulatory obligations.

#### 1.1.1 Security Control Objectives

| Security Objective | Control Requirement | Expected Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Identity Protection | Authenticate users and protect privileged identities | Reduce unauthorized access |
| Access Governance | Enforce least privilege and appropriate data access | Prevent excessive permissions |
| Data Protection | Protect sensitive information throughout its lifecycle | Reduce data exposure |
| Application Security | Secure custom applications and business logic | Reduce application vulnerabilities |
| Integration Security | Protect APIs, credentials and connected systems | Prevent unauthorized data exchange |
| Monitoring and Detection | Identify suspicious activity and security violations | Improve threat detection |
| Incident Management | Respond to security incidents effectively | Minimize business impact |
| Configuration Governance | Control changes to Salesforce environments | Reduce configuration-related risks |
| Third-Party Security | Assess external applications and service providers | Reduce supply-chain exposure |
| Operational Resilience | Maintain critical business services | Reduce disruption |

#### 1.1.2 Defense-in-Depth Architecture

Salesforce security should not depend on a single security mechanism.

A defense-in-depth approach implements multiple layers of protection so that the failure of one control does not automatically result in a successful compromise.

The proposed security layers include:

1. Identity and authentication security.
2. User authorization and access management.
3. Salesforce application security.
4. Data protection and governance.
5. API and integration security.
6. Endpoint and network security.
7. Security monitoring and incident response.
8. Governance, risk and compliance oversight.

For example, multi-factor authentication can reduce the likelihood of unauthorized account access. However, if an attacker compromises an authenticated session, additional controls such as least privilege, session monitoring and data access restrictions remain important.

### 2. Preventive Security Controls

Preventive controls are designed to stop unauthorized access, malicious activity, data exposure and security violations before they occur.

#### 2.1.1 Identity and Authentication Controls

Identity security is a fundamental requirement because Salesforce users and connected applications authenticate before accessing protected resources.

Recommended controls include:

- Enforce multi-factor authentication in accordance with Salesforce requirements and the organization's authentication architecture.
- Implement single sign-on through an approved enterprise identity provider.
- Apply strong authentication policies.
- Use phishing-resistant authentication where feasible.
- Restrict privileged account access.
- Implement account lifecycle management.
- Disable or remove unnecessary accounts.
- Apply appropriate session security settings.
- Review authentication policies periodically.
- Monitor unusual authentication activities.

**Salesforce implementation considerations:**

The organization should evaluate Salesforce-supported authentication methods, identity provider configurations, session policies and connected application authentication.

Authentication requirements must be validated against the organization's Salesforce edition, configuration and current product capabilities.

#### 2.1.2 Authorization and Access Control

Authentication confirms the identity of a user or application. Authorization determines what that identity is permitted to access and perform.

Salesforce provides several access control mechanisms that must be configured according to business requirements.

| Salesforce Security Mechanism | Security Purpose |
|---|---|
| Organization-Wide Defaults | Establish baseline record-level access |
| Role Hierarchy | Provides record access based on organizational hierarchy, subject to configuration |
| Sharing Rules | Extend record access to defined groups of users |
| Manual Sharing | Provides record access through specific sharing arrangements |
| Profiles | Define baseline user permissions and settings |
| Permission Sets | Grant additional permissions to users |
| Permission Set Groups | Combine permission sets into logical access packages |
| Object Permissions | Control access to Salesforce objects |
| Field-Level Security | Restrict access to individual fields |
| Record-Level Security | Controls access to specific records |

Recommended implementation practices:

1. Define access requirements based on business roles.
2. Establish restrictive organization-wide defaults where appropriate.
3. Design role hierarchies according to actual business requirements.
4. Apply sharing rules only where justified.
5. Use permission sets and permission set groups to manage additional access.
6. Restrict access to sensitive objects and fields.
7. Review privileged permissions.
8. Implement periodic access certification.
9. Remove unnecessary permissions promptly.
10. Maintain evidence of access approvals and reviews.

The organization must avoid treating role hierarchy as a universal substitute for record-level security design. Salesforce access is determined by the combined effect of several security mechanisms.

#### 2.1.3 Privileged Access Management

Salesforce administrators can perform activities that significantly affect platform security and business operations.

Examples include:

- Managing user accounts.
- Assigning permissions.
- Modifying security configurations.
- Creating or modifying connected applications.
- Managing automation.
- Deploying application changes.
- Accessing sensitive information.

Recommended privileged access controls include:

- Dedicated administrator accounts where appropriate.
- Strong authentication.
- Least-privilege administrative permissions.
- Separation of duties.
- Approval workflows for privileged access.
- Periodic privileged access reviews.
- Monitoring of administrative activities.
- Controlled emergency access.
- Prompt revocation of unnecessary privileges.

Where the organization operates an enterprise Privileged Access Management (PAM) solution, Salesforce administrative access should be evaluated for integration with the broader privileged identity governance model.

#### 2.1.4 Data Protection Controls

Salesforce environments may contain personal information, customer records, financial information and confidential business data.

Preventive data protection controls should include:

- Data classification.
- Data minimization.
- Appropriate object and field permissions.
- Record-level access restrictions.
- Secure data transfer.
- Protection of sensitive credentials.
- Controlled data exports.
- Secure handling of files and attachments.
- Appropriate data retention.
- Encryption where required and supported.

**Salesforce Shield considerations:**

Salesforce Shield provides additional security capabilities that may support enterprise data protection requirements.

Its capabilities include:

- Platform Encryption.
- Event Monitoring.
- Field Audit Trail.

The organization must verify product licensing, feature availability, supported data types, configuration requirements and technical limitations before treating these capabilities as implemented controls.

Encryption should not be treated as a replacement for access control. An authorized user or compromised account may still access information through legitimate application functionality.

#### 2.1.5 Application Security Controls

Custom Salesforce applications introduce development-related security responsibilities.

Preventive controls should include:

- Secure coding standards.
- Secure Apex development.
- Appropriate authorization checks.
- Input validation.
- Secure query construction.
- Protection against injection vulnerabilities.
- Secure Lightning Web Component implementation.
- Appropriate handling of user-controlled data.
- Secure error handling.
- Dependency and package assessment.
- Peer code reviews.
- Secure development lifecycle requirements.

Developers must understand Salesforce-specific execution contexts and sharing behavior.

For example, Apex code can operate with different security characteristics depending on how it is implemented. Developers must explicitly evaluate record sharing, object permissions and field-level security rather than assume that all access restrictions are automatically enforced in every execution context.

#### 2.1.6 API and Integration Security Controls

APIs allow Salesforce to exchange data with internal and external applications.

The integration architecture must protect both the data being exchanged and the identities authorized to perform integration operations.

Recommended controls include:

- Use approved authentication mechanisms.
- Apply least-privilege OAuth scopes.
- Create dedicated integration identities.
- Restrict integration permissions.
- Protect credentials through approved secrets management.
- Avoid hardcoded credentials.
- Apply appropriate credential rotation and revocation procedures.
- Validate incoming data.
- Protect API endpoints.
- Monitor API usage.
- Establish controls against excessive or unauthorized data extraction.

For integrations using MuleSoft or other middleware, the organization must assess security across both the Salesforce endpoint and the integration platform.

#### 2.1.7 Third-Party Application Controls

Third-party applications may introduce additional permissions, data processing activities and external dependencies.

Preventive controls should include:

- Security assessment before installation.
- Business justification and application ownership.
- Review of requested permissions.
- OAuth scope assessment.
- Vendor security due diligence.
- Privacy and data processing review.
- Contractual security requirements.
- Approval of production deployment.
- Periodic reassessment.
- Removal of unnecessary connected applications.

An application being available through AppExchange does not eliminate the organization's responsibility to assess its suitability for the intended use case.

### 3. Detective Security Controls

Detective controls identify suspicious activities, unauthorized access, security violations and abnormal system behavior.

Detection capabilities are particularly important in SaaS environments because organizations may not have direct access to the underlying cloud infrastructure.

#### 3.1.1 Salesforce Security Monitoring

Salesforce security monitoring should provide visibility into activities that may indicate compromise or misuse.

Examples include:

- Failed authentication attempts.
- Unusual login locations or patterns.
- Privileged account activities.
- Permission changes.
- Changes to sharing configurations.
- Connected application activity.
- API usage anomalies.
- Bulk data exports.
- Unusual record access.
- Changes to critical business configurations.

The organization must determine which monitoring capabilities are available through its Salesforce products and licenses.

#### 3.1.2 Salesforce Event Monitoring

Salesforce Shield includes Event Monitoring capabilities that can provide visibility into supported Salesforce user and system activities.

Depending on the applicable subscription and available event types, monitoring may support investigation of:

- User activity.
- Login activity.
- API activity.
- Data access.
- Report exports.
- Administrative operations.
- Application usage.

Event availability, delivery mechanisms, retention and access to event data must be verified against current Salesforce documentation and licensing.

Event Monitoring should be assessed as part of the enterprise logging and monitoring architecture.

#### 3.1.3 Salesforce Setup Audit Trail

Setup Audit Trail provides visibility into supported administrative configuration changes.

It can help security and platform teams investigate activities such as:

- Changes to user permissions.
- Modifications to security settings.
- Changes to configuration.
- Administrative operations.
- Other supported setup activities.

Recommended controls include:

1. Review administrative configuration changes.
2. Identify unauthorized or unexpected modifications.
3. Correlate changes with approved change records.
4. Investigate changes made outside approved maintenance windows.
5. Retain relevant evidence according to organizational requirements.

Setup Audit Trail is not a substitute for comprehensive application logging or security monitoring.

#### 3.1.4 Identity and Access Monitoring

The enterprise identity provider should monitor authentication activities associated with Salesforce.

Detection use cases may include:

- Repeated failed login attempts.
- Unusual authentication locations.
- Suspicious authentication patterns.
- Unexpected privileged access.
- Abnormal session activity.
- Access from unmanaged or non-compliant devices, where supported.
- Suspicious OAuth application activity.

Security teams should correlate identity provider events with Salesforce activity where sufficient data is available.

#### 3.1.5 Data Access and Exfiltration Monitoring

The organization should monitor activities that could indicate unauthorized data extraction.

Examples include:

- Large-volume report exports.
- Unusual API extraction activity.
- Repeated access to sensitive records.
- Unexpected downloads of files.
- Abnormal access outside normal business patterns.
- Unusual activity from privileged accounts.

Detection thresholds should be based on normal business behavior and should avoid generating excessive false positives.

### 4. Corrective and Recovery Controls

Corrective and recovery controls reduce the impact of security incidents and restore normal operations.

These controls should address unauthorized access, data compromise, malicious configuration changes, application vulnerabilities and service disruptions.

#### 4.1.1 Account Compromise Response

If a Salesforce account is suspected of being compromised, the organization should have procedures to:

1. Validate the security alert.
2. Identify the affected user or integration identity.
3. Revoke or disable compromised access as appropriate.
4. Invalidate active sessions or tokens where supported.
5. Reset credentials and restore secure authentication.
6. Review recent account activity.
7. Identify affected records and connected applications.
8. Determine whether data was accessed or extracted.
9. Preserve investigation evidence.
10. Restore access only after the account is secured.

For compromised integration identities, the response must also consider connected middleware and dependent enterprise applications.

#### 4.1.2 Unauthorized Configuration Change Response

Unauthorized changes to Salesforce security settings can weaken the entire environment.

Examples include:

- Changes to administrator permissions.
- Modification of sharing rules.
- Changes to authentication settings.
- Creation of unauthorized connected applications.
- Modification of integration permissions.
- Changes to critical automation.

Corrective actions should include:

- Identifying the unauthorized change.
- Determining the responsible identity.
- Comparing the configuration against the approved baseline.
- Reverting unauthorized changes.
- Validating security settings.
- Reviewing related activities.
- Documenting the incident.
- Updating preventive controls where necessary.

#### 4.1.3 Data Incident Response

When customer or business data is suspected to have been exposed, the organization should:

- Identify the affected information.
- Determine the scope of exposure.
- Identify the affected users and systems.
- Establish whether data was accessed, modified or extracted.
- Contain ongoing unauthorized access.
- Preserve relevant evidence.
- Involve privacy and legal stakeholders where required.
- Assess notification obligations.
- Implement remediation.
- Document lessons learned.

The response must be coordinated with the organization's incident response and data breach management procedures.

#### 4.1.4 Data Recovery and Restoration

Data recovery capabilities should be designed according to business requirements.

Potential recovery mechanisms include:

- Salesforce-supported data export and recovery services.
- Approved third-party backup solutions.
- Data restoration procedures.
- Metadata backup and deployment controls.
- Recovery of connected application data.
- Reconciliation of affected transactions.

The organization should not assume that Salesforce service availability automatically provides the required customer-level data recovery capability.

Backup, retention, recovery point objectives and recovery time objectives must be evaluated separately.

### 6. Technical Security Configuration

Technical security configuration translates security requirements into enforceable Salesforce settings and operational controls.

Configuration decisions should be documented, approved, tested and periodically reviewed.

#### 6.1.1 User and Permission Configuration

Recommended configuration practices include:

- Assign minimum required permissions.
- Avoid unnecessary administrative privileges.
- Use permission sets for additional access.
- Group permissions according to business functions.
- Restrict access to sensitive objects.
- Apply field-level security.
- Review record-sharing requirements.
- Remove unused permission assignments.
- Establish approval processes for privileged access.

**Implementation example:**

A customer service representative requires access to customer contact information and support cases but does not require access to financial reporting or administrative configuration.

The access model should grant the representative the permissions required to perform customer support activities without providing unnecessary access to unrelated business functions.

#### 6.1.2 Organization-Wide Default Configuration

Organization-wide defaults establish baseline record access.

The organization should evaluate each relevant object and determine the appropriate default access model.

Configuration considerations include:

- Public Read/Write.
- Public Read Only.
- Private.
- Controlled by Parent, where supported and applicable.

The selected configuration must reflect business requirements and the sensitivity of the information.

A restrictive default can reduce unnecessary access, but overly restrictive settings can also disrupt legitimate business processes.

The correct design must balance confidentiality and operational requirements.

#### 6.1.3 Field-Level Security Configuration

Field-level security controls whether users can view or edit individual fields.

It is particularly important for sensitive information such as:

- Government identification information.
- Customer verification attributes.
- Financial information.
- Confidential customer details.
- Internal risk classifications.
- Sensitive business information.

Recommended practices include:

1. Identify sensitive fields.
2. Define approved access roles.
3. Configure field-level permissions.
4. Validate access through representative user accounts.
5. Review permissions periodically.
6. Document approved exceptions.

#### 6.1.4 Session and Login Configuration

The organization should evaluate available session and authentication settings according to its identity architecture.

Relevant considerations include:

- Session timeout requirements.
- Session security.
- Login restrictions.
- Authentication policies.
- Trusted network configuration, where applicable.
- Device and endpoint security requirements.
- Single sign-on behavior.
- Emergency access procedures.

The organization should avoid treating IP-based restrictions as a complete security solution because users, devices and applications may operate across distributed environments.

#### 6.1.5 Secure Development Configuration

Custom Salesforce development should follow approved security requirements.

Recommended controls include:

- Secure Apex coding practices.
- Secure Lightning Web Component development.
- Appropriate execution context selection.
- Explicit authorization checks.
- Input validation.
- Secure query construction.
- Peer review.
- Static code analysis.
- Controlled deployment.
- Security testing before production release.

Security requirements should be incorporated into the software development lifecycle rather than introduced only during final testing.

The enterprise IAM architecture should provide centralized identity governance and authentication where supported.

Relevant integrations include:

- Access certification.

**Security objective:**

Centralize identity governance and reduce the risk of unmanaged or excessive access to Salesforce.

#### 8.1.2 SIEM Integration

A Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) platform collects and correlates security events from multiple systems.

Salesforce-related security data may be incorporated into the enterprise SIEM using supported event delivery mechanisms, APIs, connectors or integration services.

Potential data sources include:

- Salesforce Event Monitoring data.
- Login and authentication events.
- Setup Audit Trail information.

- Investigation of unauthorized configuration changes.

#### 8.1.3 XDR Integration

Extended Detection and Response (XDR) platforms correlate security telemetry across supported endpoints, identities, applications and other security sources.

Salesforce-related telemetry may contribute to broader threat investigation where supported by the selected XDR platform and integration architecture.

Potential use cases include:

- Correlating a compromised employee endpoint with suspicious Salesforce authentication.
- Investigating identity compromise across multiple enterprise services.
- Connecting endpoint malware alerts with suspicious CRM access.
- Supporting incident investigation across cloud and endpoint environments.

XDR capabilities vary by vendor. The organization must validate whether Salesforce telemetry is directly supported or must be integrated through a SIEM, API or another intermediary.

#### 8.1.4 SOAR Integration

Security Orchestration, Automation and Response (SOAR) platforms coordinate security workflows and automate approved response actions.

Potential Salesforce-related SOAR use cases include:

- Enriching suspicious login alerts.
- Creating security incident tickets.
- Notifying Salesforce platform administrators.
- Initiating investigation workflows.
- Coordinating access revocation through approved identity integrations.
- Escalating suspected data exposure.
- Tracking remediation activities.

Automated actions must be carefully controlled.

For example, automatically disabling an integration identity could interrupt critical business processes. Response playbooks should therefore include business impact checks and appropriate authorization.

#### 8.1.5 EDR Integration

Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR) protects supported endpoints against malicious activity.

EDR does not directly replace Salesforce application security controls.

However, it can provide important contextual information when investigating a compromised device used to access Salesforce.

Potential use cases include:

- Identifying malware on a user's endpoint.
- Investigating suspicious browser activity.
- Correlating endpoint compromise with Salesforce access.
- Supporting containment of compromised devices.
- Providing endpoint forensic evidence.

#### 8.1.6 Data Loss Prevention Integration

Data Loss Prevention (DLP) technologies help identify and control sensitive information movement across supported channels.

Potential Salesforce-related use cases include:

- Monitoring sensitive data exports.
- Identifying unauthorized transfers.
- Detecting sensitive information in files.
- Applying endpoint or cloud data protection policies.
- Investigating data leakage incidents.

The organization must verify the capabilities of its DLP solution and the integration methods available for Salesforce.

### 11. Implementation Procedures

Security controls must be implemented through a structured process to ensure that they are correctly designed, approved, tested and maintained.

#### 11.1.1 Security Control Implementation Lifecycle

The proposed implementation lifecycle consists of the following stages:

1. Identify the security requirement: Determine the risk or business requirement that requires a control.
2. Define the control objective: Establish what the control must prevent, detect or correct.
3. Select the control: Determine the appropriate Salesforce-native or enterprise security capability.
4. Assign ownership: Identify the implementation owner and accountable control owner.
5. Design the configuration: Define the technical settings and operational procedures.
6. Obtain approval: Secure appropriate business and security approval.
7. Implement the control: Configure the relevant Salesforce or enterprise technology.
8. Test the implementation: Validate that the control operates as intended.
9. Document evidence: Retain configuration records, approvals and test results.
10. Monitor effectiveness: Review control performance and address identified weaknesses.

#### 11.1.2 Example: Implementing Least-Privilege Access

Objective: Ensure that Salesforce users receive only the permissions required to perform their assigned business responsibilities.

Implementation procedure:

1. Identify the business roles requiring Salesforce access.
2. Document the objects and records required by each role.
3. Define required object-level permissions.
4. Identify sensitive fields requiring restrictions.
5. Review organization-wide defaults.
6. Review sharing rules and role hierarchy.
7. Design permission sets and permission set groups.
8. Obtain business owner approval.
9. Implement the configuration in an appropriate test environment.
10. Validate access using representative test accounts.
11. Deploy approved changes to production.
12. Retain access configuration and approval evidence.
13. Schedule periodic access reviews.

Expected evidence:

- Approved access matrix.
- User access request records.
- Permission configuration.
- Test results.
- Access review reports.
- Remediation records.

#### 11.1.3 Example: Implementing Salesforce SIEM Monitoring

Objective: Improve detection of suspicious Salesforce activities through centralized security monitoring.

Implementation procedure:

1. Identify security monitoring requirements.
2. Determine available Salesforce event sources.
3. Verify required Salesforce licensing and event availability.
4. Identify supported event delivery mechanisms.
5. Define the SIEM integration architecture.
6. Configure the approved integration.
7. Validate event collection.
8. Define relevant detection use cases.
9. Configure alert thresholds and severity.
10. Test alert generation and investigation workflows.
11. Assign monitoring responsibilities.
12. Retain evidence of successful event ingestion and alert testing.

Expected evidence:

- Approved monitoring architecture.
- Event source inventory.
- Integration configuration.
- SIEM ingestion records.
- Detection rule documentation.
- Alert test results.
- Incident investigation records.

| Incident Response    | Security Operations           | CISO or Delegated Security Authority | Incident response records and investigation evidence |

#### 12.1.4 Control Effectiveness Requirements

A control should not be considered effective merely because it has been configured.

The organization must establish evidence that the control:

- Addresses an identified risk.
- Is appropriately designed.
- Has been implemented.
- Operates consistently.
- Is monitored.
- Is reviewed periodically.
- Produces the intended security outcome.

For example, assigning users restricted permissions is only one part of access governance.

The organization must also demonstrate that permissions are approved, reviewed, updated when responsibilities change and removed when access is no longer required.

This distinction is essential for effective Salesforce cybersecurity governance and risk management.
